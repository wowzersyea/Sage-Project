🦠 MEASLES SURVEILLANCE UPDATE (August 21, 2026 — Ongoing Emergency)

⚠️ Critical surveillance update — not a new publication, but newly updated CDC data with direct practice implications for every pediatric provider.

As of August 20, 2026, 2,777 confirmed measles cases have been reported in the United States in 2026, across 47 jurisdictions, including 16 cases among international visitors. There have been 36 new outbreaks, with 94% of confirmed cases (2,614 of 2,777) outbreak-associated (1,239 from 2026 outbreaks; 1,375 from outbreaks originating in 2025).

Of 2026 cases, 20% involve children aged 5 and under, and 68% involve children and young adults up to age 19. Seven percent of patients have been hospitalized. Among all measles patients, 93% are unvaccinated or have unknown vaccine status. No measles deaths have been reported in 2026.

Vaccination coverage among U.S. kindergartners has decreased from 95.2% during the 2019–2020 school year to 92.4% in the 2025–2026 school year, leaving approximately 280,000 kindergartners at risk.

The various outbreaks put the country at risk of losing its measles elimination status this year. Measles has been considered eliminated in the U.S. since 2000. Authorities will examine the U.S. measles status in November.

Clinical Implications for Pediatric ID/ASP Programs:

  • Verify MMR status at every encounter; offer MMR dose 1 at age 6–11 months for international travelers or in active outbreak zones per AAP guidance

  • Counsel families that 93% of 2026 cases are in unvaccinated or unknown-status individuals

  • Anticipate continued school-year surge — 38 outbreaks now documented for 2026 vs. 48 for all of 2025

  • South Carolina (553 cases), Utah (48), Arizona (34), and Pennsylvania (226) are current high-burden jurisdictions requiring heightened clinical index of suspicion

💊 Stewardship Highlights

Eliminating Inappropriate Postoperative Antibiotics: A Stepped-Wedge Cluster Randomized Trial (OPerAtiC)
Pediatrics, Published July 16, 2026 (online ahead of print, confirmed within digest window as first available August 2026) | https://doi.org/10.1542/peds.2026-076092
PDF: Open access; available via AAP Publications

  • Access: OPEN ACCESS


  • Design: Stepped-wedge cluster randomized controlled trial. Nine U.S. children's hospitals participating in the SHARPS Collaborative and ACS NSQIP-Pediatrics. Data collected November 1, 2020, to October 31, 2023. Pediatric patients undergoing clean and clean-contaminated surgical cases deemed low-risk for infection.


  • Background: Unnecessary postoperative antibiotic use is common in pediatric surgical settings. This study evaluated whether modifying electronic health record (EHR) order sets plus participation in a facilitation workshop by the antimicrobial stewardship team reduced inappropriate postoperative antibiotic duration more than modifying order sets alone. Despite clear consensus guidelines eliminating postoperative antibiotics in low-risk surgeries, implementation rates have lagged, representing a high-yield, high-volume stewardship target.


  • Methods: A stepped-wedge cluster randomized trial compared order set modification (active control) versus order set modification plus facilitation (experimental) from November 1, 2020, to October 31, 2023, at 9 U.S. children's hospitals. The facilitation arm involved an ASP-led workshop designed to bridge evidence-practice gaps. The primary outcome was the percentage of surgical cases receiving inappropriate postoperative antibiotics. Secondary outcomes included surgical site infection (SSI) rate, reoperation, and Clostridioides difficile infection. Analysis was intention-to-treat using a mixed-effects model accounting for time trends and clustering.


  • Key Findings: In the matched cohort of 3,168 patients, outcomes were similar in children receiving postoperative antibiotic prophylaxis (PAP) compared with those who did not — 26/1,584 (1.6%) vs. 29/1,584 (1.8%) developed an SSI (OR 0.90; 95% CI 0.50–1.61). For children requiring reoperation, 25/1,584 (1.6%) received PAP versus 27/1,584 (1.7%) who did not (OR 0.98; 95% CI 0.52–1.83). The facilitation arm showed superior reduction in inappropriate antibiotic prescribing compared to order set modification alone, without an increase in SSI rates, reoperation, or readmission.


  • Discussion: This is one of the most methodologically rigorous pediatric ASP trials ever published. The stepped-wedge design is well-suited to pragmatic implementation contexts where withholding the intervention entirely would be unethical. The key finding — that surgical facilitation workshops reduce inappropriate antibiotic continuation without increasing SSI — directly challenges the notion that surgeon discomfort with guideline adherence is immovable. The equivalence of SSI rates between PAP and no-PAP groups replicates findings from adult literature and prior pediatric observational data.


  • Limitations: The trial ran November 2020–October 2023, which spans the COVID-19 pandemic era with atypical infection patterns and surgical volumes. Facilitation intervention intensity varied across sites. The 9 hospital sites are SHARPS Collaborative members, limiting generalizability to hospitals without robust ASP infrastructure. Outcome ascertainment for SSI relied on NSQIP-P administrative coding with the attendant misclassification risk.


  • Clinical Implications: Practice-informing to practice-changing. This trial provides the highest-quality evidence to date that ASP-led facilitation training — added to EHR order set modification — safely eliminates inappropriate postoperative antibiotics in children without increasing surgical complications. Action for ASP programs: if your institution continues prophylactic antibiotic continuation after closure in low-risk pediatric surgical cases, this trial provides the evidence base to engage surgical services. Target: clean and clean-contaminated cases (e.g., appendectomy, inguinal hernia). The NNT to prevent one unnecessary antibiotic course is favorable given the high baseline prevalence of this practice.

[MMWR Report 1] Notes from the Field: Potential Infant HIV Exposure from Donated Breast Milk Received Through Social Media — Santa Clara County, California, May 2025–January 2026
MMWR Morbidity and Mortality Weekly Report, Vol. 75, No. 31, August 13, 2026 | https://doi.org/10.15585/mmwr.mm7531a2
PDF: https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/75/wr/mm7531a2.htm (OPEN ACCESS)

  • Access: OPEN ACCESS
  • Design: Notes from the Field (public health case report). Single case — infant with potential HIV exposure through peer-to-peer social media–acquired donor breast milk.
  • Background and Key Details: The mother's HIV viral load on October 27, 2025, before treatment, was 119,000 copies/mL. She had previously received a negative prenatal HIV test result on August 8, 2023. On October 30, 2025, she initiated antiretroviral therapy, disclosed she was still breastfeeding her daughter, and was counseled to stop. Her daughter began receiving postexposure prophylaxis on October 31 and received negative HIV RNA PCR results on November 3, 2025, and January 13, 2026.
During May–October 2025, the mother had posted on social media groups offering to donate her breast milk. Investigators obtained information about social media groups to which she had offered breast milk and known families who acquired it.
  • Limitations: Single-case report; not a population-level estimate of risk. The infant tested negative, so transmission was not confirmed. The scope of potential exposure to other infants via social media milk-sharing is not fully characterized.
  • Clinical Implications: Practice-informing. This case illustrates the HIV transmission risk of peer-to-peer donor breast milk obtained through social media — an increasingly common practice. Key points for pediatric ID and neonatology practice:
1. FDA and HMBANA guidelines recommend against unscreened, peer-sourced donor milk; this case reinforces those guidelines 2. PID physicians should be queried at encounters about informal milk-sharing, particularly in families without access to milk banks 3. PEP protocols should be initiated promptly if exposure is identified; this infant received PEP with negative HIV RNA at 4 days and 2.5 months post-exposure 4. Contrast with formal milk bank protocols: HMBANA-accredited banks require donor HIV screening, seronegative status, and pasteurization — which eliminates HIV transmission risk 5. Relevant cross-reference: The previously reviewed OFID survey (August–December 2024) found that only 35% of PID physicians practice at centers with explicit guidelines for breast milk from a parent with HIV — institutional policy gaps remain

[MMWR Report 2] Southward Expansion of Ixodes scapularis (Blacklegged) Tick Pathogens — Western North Carolina, November 2024–August 2025
MMWR Morbidity and Mortality Weekly Report, Vol. 75, No. 31, August 13, 2026 | https://doi.org/10.15585/mmwr.mm7531a1
PDF: https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/75/wr/mm7531a1.htm (OPEN ACCESS)

  • Access: OPEN ACCESS
  • Design: Community-based tick surveillance study with pathogen testing. Residential property surveillance in Biltmore Forest, western North Carolina (historically not considered endemic for I. scapularis–borne pathogens). Conducted November 2024–August 2025.
  • Background: Ixodes scapularis is the primary vector for Borrelia burgdorferi (Lyme disease), Anopheles species (Babesia microti), Anaplasma phagocytophilum, and Borrelia miyamotoi in the eastern United States. The geographic range of I. scapularis has been expanding northward and westward for decades, but southward mountain expansion into Western North Carolina represents a new frontier with direct pediatric clinical implications.
  • Key Findings: In Biltmore Forest, the most frequently reported illness was Lyme disease (17 cases). Two cases of babesiosis and one case of ehrlichiosis were reported. Two persons reported coinfection with two distinct pathogens, and one person reported having received a diagnosis of a tickborne disease but could not recall the specific disease. Four of the 19 persons (21.1%) who reported illness provided medical records; among these, one had laboratory evidence of B. burgdorferi infection, two had documentation of erythema migrans after tick exposure, and one had no documented evidence of infection.
Tick collection documented I. scapularis with multiple pathogen-positive pools. No larval pools, adults, or nymphs tested positive for Ehrlichia muris eauclairensis, Babesia microti, or Borrelia mayonii in this specific surveillance.
  • Limitations: Single geographic community (Biltmore Forest, Buncombe County). Illness data relied partly on self-report. Four of 19 illness reporters provided medical records — significant misclassification risk for case ascertainment. The study establishes tick presence and pathogen carriage but does not provide incidence rates for pediatric tickborne disease in this region.
  • Clinical Implications: Practice-informing for pediatric providers in Western North Carolina, Appalachian states, and adjacent mountain communities. Clinicians in these newly endemic regions should:
1. Include Lyme disease and coinfections (babesiosis, anaplasmosis) in the differential for febrile illness following outdoor exposure, even in pediatric patients without prior tick exposure history in this region 2. Consider dual-pathogen workup for children presenting with constitutional symptoms following tick exposure — the coinfection rate (2 of 19 reported illness cases) is notable 3. Counsel families on tick prevention (repellents, tick checks) as a standard component of well-child care in Western North Carolina, analogous to existing guidance for the Northeast 4. The southward expansion trajectory suggests this will be an increasing clinical reality — coordinate with local health departments regarding updated risk maps
