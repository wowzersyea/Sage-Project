⚠️ BUNDIBUGYO VIRUS (EBOLA) PHEIC — MAJOR OUTBREAK ESCALATION

This is not a new publication, but a mandatory situational update with direct implications for U.S. pediatric ID/ASP clinical practice. The outbreak has changed dramatically since our last digest.

Status as of July 9–10, 2026 (ECDC/WHO/CDC):

As of July 9, 2026, the DRC has reported a total of 1,792 confirmed cases, including 625 confirmed deaths; 764 patients are currently hospitalized in isolation. This represents a roughly 5-fold increase from the 378 cases reported at the time of the prior MMWR analysis (June 2, 2026). As of July 10, 2026, the outbreak is affecting DRC and Uganda, with 33 new confirmed cases and 25 deaths in the most recent reporting interval, spreading from Ituri into North Kivu province.

As of July 1, a cumulative 1,460 confirmed DRC cases (including 452 deaths) had been reported, with Uganda confirming 20 cases including two deaths. On June 24, French authorities notified WHO of a laboratory-confirmed Bundibugyo case in a medical doctor returning from DRC — the first confirmed case in a high-income country. The outbreak is caused by the Bundibugyo ebolavirus, which has complicated response efforts because existing Ebola treatments were certified for a different Ebola species, the Zaire ebolavirus.

Therapeutics update: WHO has recommended three candidate agents for clinical trials: the antiviral remdesivir and the monoclonal antibody therapies MBP-134 and maftivimab; trials of remdesivir and MBP-134 started in DRC in July 2026. For prevention, three potential vaccines are to be expedited: candidates under development by IAVI, Moderna, and the University of Oxford. None is approved or available for clinical use.

U.S. pediatric practice implications:

  • On May 18, 2026, CDC, DHS, and other federal agencies implemented public health entry screening and entry restrictions; an Order continuing suspension of entry of specified foreign nationals was issued June 21, 2026, effective for 30 days.

  • To date, no cases of Ebola disease have been confirmed in the United States; the overall risk to the American public and travelers remains low.

  • Any child presenting with hemorrhagic fever syndrome and travel history to DRC/Uganda/affected areas within 21 days requires immediate isolation, infectious disease consultation, and public health notification. Supportive care only — no approved vaccine or treatment exists.

  • Clinical trials of remdesivir and MBP-134 are ongoing in DRC; data are expected later in 2026.


Case-fatality rate update: 625 deaths/1,792 confirmed cases = ~34.9% CFR — substantially higher than the prior reports' ~16.7% (63/378), consistent with late-stage outbreak healthcare system saturation and community transmission.

⚠️ U.S. MEASLES EMERGENCY — ACTIVE & WORSENING

As of July 9, 2026, 2,231 confirmed measles cases were reported in the United States in 2026. There have been 32 new outbreaks reported in 2026, and 93% of confirmed cases (2,082 of 2,231) are outbreak-associated. Measles infections in the U.S. this year are on track to soon eclipse 2025, when the country reported the most annual cases in over three decades. CDC has warned state and local health departments that more cases are likely to come with summer travel and large gatherings.

Regional context for Americas: The Region of the Americas has reported 479 new confirmed measles cases across seven countries during epidemiologic weeks 23–24 of 2026, though this represents a 51.1% decrease compared to the previous two-week period, which had 980 new cases; 39 deaths have been reported regionally.

Safety and Efficacy of Imipenem/Cilastatin/Relebactam in Children and Neonates: Results of a Multinational Phase 2/3 Trial
The Pediatric Infectious Disease Journal, Vol. 45(7), Published Online July 7, 2026 | https://doi.org/10.1097/INF.0000000000005XXX (PIDJ July 2026, online ahead of issue)

  • Access: PAYWALLED

  • Design: Open-label, randomized, active-controlled, multinational Phase 2/3 trial (NCT03969901). Children birth to <18 years. Three indication cohorts: HABP/VABP, complicated intra-abdominal infection (cIAI), and complicated urinary tract infection (cUTI) including pyelonephritis.

  • Background: IMI/REL is a fixed-dose combination of imipenem/cilastatin with the β-lactamase inhibitor relebactam with broad gram-negative coverage; prospectively obtained safety and efficacy data in neonates and children were identified as a critical gap. The prior Phase 1b PK data (Bradley et al., J Clin Pharmacol 2023) had established that imipenem, cilastatin, and relebactam each have a generally similar drug exposure profile across pediatric age cohorts, with PK/PD targets exceeded at modified age- and weight-adjusted doses, informing dose selection for this definitive trial.

  • Methods: Children (birth to <18 years) with HABP/VABP, cIAI, and cUTI (including pyelonephritis) were enrolled in this open-label, randomized, active-controlled, multinational Phase 2/3 trial (NCT03969901). Participants were randomized 3:1 to IMI/REL or active-control standard-of-care antibacterial therapy for 7–14 days (HABP/VABP; IV only) or 5–14 days (cIAI, cUTI; with oral step-down permitted after 3 days of IV). Of note, the microbiological modified intent-to-treat (mMITT) population from related susceptibility data: this Phase 2/3 open-label, randomized, active-controlled, multinational study enrolled children (birth to <18 years) with confirmed or suspected HABP/VABP, cIAI, or cUTI; participants requiring hospitalization and IV antibacterial therapy were randomized 3:1 to IMI/REL or active control.

  • Key Findings (from abstract): Full efficacy/safety results are available by subscription. From the companion susceptibility/microbiology data published alongside this trial: of 115 participants randomized, 90 were in the mMITT population; MIC distributions from clinical trial and surveillance isolates were similar. IMI-nonsusceptible baseline pathogens included 7 Enterobacterales and 3 P. aeruginosa isolates; of 4 IMI-NS Enterobacterales, 3 were susceptible to IMI/REL (those carrying KPC, AmpC, or no acquired β-lactamase), while the NDM-1–carrying isolate was not susceptible; of 3 IMI-NS P. aeruginosa isolates, 1 was susceptible to IMI/REL.

  • Discussion: This represents the first prospective pediatric efficacy data for IMI/REL across the full pediatric age spectrum. The drug combination specifically addresses KPC- and AmpC-producing Gram-negative pathogens, which are a growing challenge in pediatric ICU settings. The finding that NDM-1–producing Enterobacterales remain resistant to IMI/REL is critical — these isolates would require alternative agents (e.g., ceftazidime-avibactam + aztreonam, or meropenem-vaborbactam). Prior to this trial, IMI/REL use in children was based entirely on PK modeling and adult safety data.

  • Limitations: 3:1 randomization ratio is unusual and reduces power for the control arm; active-controlled (not placebo-controlled) design appropriate for severe infections but limits interpretation; the small number of carbapenem-resistant isolates in this relatively small pediatric study (n=115 randomized) limits conclusions about efficacy specifically against resistant pathogens, which is the drug's primary value proposition.

  • Clinical Implications: Practice-informing. This provides the first formal prospective pediatric safety and efficacy data for IMI/REL, supporting regulatory approval efforts for pediatric labeling. IMI/REL fills an important niche for ESBL- and KPC-producing Gram-negative infections in children when ceftazidime-avibactam is unavailable or not appropriate. Key caveat: NDM-producing organisms remain resistant — molecular characterization of resistance mechanisms at the time of susceptibility testing is essential to guide therapy.

Viral Coinfection as a Virus-Specific Effect Modifier in Pediatric Lower Respiratory Tract Disease Severity: A Retrospective Cohort Study
The Pediatric Infectious Disease Journal, Published Online June 29, 2026 | https://doi.org/10.1097/INF.0000000000005XXX (PIDJ advance online)

  • Access: PAYWALLED

  • Design: Retrospective cohort of 1,242 children <6 years hospitalized for viral LRTDs during 2022–2023 and 2023–2024.

  • Background: Predicting severity in pediatric lower respiratory tract diseases (LRTDs) remains challenging, and the role of viral coinfection is debated. The authors hypothesized that determinants of severity differ between emergency department presentation and in-hospital course, with viral coinfection acting as a virus-specific effect modifier rather than a uniform risk factor. Prior systematic reviews (2016) found that respiratory viral coinfection overall did not uniformly increase severity, but virus-specific interactions remained unclear.

  • Methods: This was a retrospective cohort of 1,242 children <6 years hospitalized for viral LRTDs during 2022–2023 and 2023–2024. Severity at ED presentation was defined as a Pediatric Respiratory Severity Score (PRESS ≥4), and in-hospital deterioration as a Clinical Severity Score (CSS >3). Multivariable logistic regression assessed independent predictors, including virus × coinfection interaction terms.

  • Key Findings: Severe ED presentation occurred in 12.0% (n=150), and severe in-hospital deterioration in 6.6% (n=82). Full interaction term statistics are subscription-restricted. The conceptual contribution — that coinfection's effect on severity is virus-pair specific rather than a uniform amplifier — is well-framed and consistent with the complex prior literature.

  • Discussion: This approach, modeling virus × coinfection interactions, is methodologically superior to prior analyses that treated all coinfections equivalently. The clinical implication is that identifying specific viral pairings (e.g., RSV+adenovirus vs. RSV+rhinovirus) may predict which hospitalized children are at highest risk of deterioration, enabling more targeted monitoring protocols. This also has ASP relevance: children presenting with severe illness due to high-risk viral pairings may be more likely to receive unnecessary antibiotics — understanding the viral etiology of severity reduces the clinical pressure to treat empirically.

  • Limitations: Single-country data; retrospective design with potential misclassification of coinfection (detection does not imply causation for the second pathogen); PRESS and CSS scoring systems may perform differently across institutions; the 2022–2024 post-pandemic period may have unusual pathogen co-circulation dynamics.

  • Clinical Implications: Practice-informing. Clinicians evaluating hospitalized children with LRTD should be cautious about attributing severity uniformly to "coinfection" — specific viral pairings appear to modulate risk differently. Full data review warranted when accessible.

Disease Spectrum of Human Metapneumovirus Infections in Infants and Young Children: Data From a Prospective Multicenter Study in Germany
The Pediatric Infectious Disease Journal, Vol. 45(7), pp. 578–582, July 2026 | https://doi.org/10.1097/INF.0000000000005166

  • Access: OPEN ACCESS (Creative Commons BY-NC-ND 4.0)

  • PDF: https://journals.lww.com/pidj/fulltext/9900/disease_spectrum_of_human_metapneumovirus.1629.aspx

  • Design: Retrospective observational analysis from a prospective multicenter surveillance cohort. Data were drawn from the Pediatric Airway Pathogen Incidence (PAPI) study, a multicenter surveillance study of lower respiratory tract infections conducted in Germany during winter seasons 2021/22 and 2022/23 (weeks 40–17 each season); 102 hospitalized and 114 outpatient pediatric cases with laboratory-confirmed hMPV infection were compared, after excluding coinfections with RSV. Setting: multicenter, Germany; retrospective analysis of prospectively collected data.

  • Background: hMPV is a significant respiratory pathogen in infants and young children; although most infections present as nonsevere cases in outpatient settings, severe courses can lead to hospitalization; few potential risk factors for hospitalization have been identified, and studies comparing clinical presentation in inpatient vs. outpatient settings are lacking. With no licensed vaccine or specific antiviral for hMPV, and with post-pandemic hMPV surges observed across multiple countries, understanding hospitalization risk factors has immediate ASP relevance (driving inappropriate antibiotic use in children with bacterial coinfection misattribution).

  • Methods: RSV coinfection was specifically excluded to isolate hMPV-specific clinical patterns. Detailed clinical and demographic data were collected prospectively in the PAPI surveillance framework; comparison was made between 102 inpatients and 114 outpatients using multivariable logistic regression (exact covariates subscription-restricted). Age, comorbidities, and clinical presentation variables were assessed.

  • Key Findings: The clinical presentation of hMPV in hospitalized young children differed significantly from that observed in outpatient settings, with multiple factors independently associated with hMPV-related hospitalization. Specific independent factors identified (from publicly accessible text): In comparison to outpatient cases, hospitalization was associated with higher occurrence of wheezing at auscultation (63.7% vs. 35.1%, P<0.001), crackles (64.7% vs. 40.4%, P<0.001), signs of increased respiratory effort with tachypnea (70.6% vs. 18.4%, P<0.001), and hypoxemia (47%... [full data subscription-restricted].

  • Clinical symptoms: Patients with hMPV infection presented with coughing, nasal congestion, and fever as well as crackles, wheezing, and tachypnea; gastrointestinal symptoms were less common.

  • Discussion: The finding that hMPV hospitalization is predicted by wheezing on auscultation, crackles, tachypnea, and hypoxemia at presentation is clinically useful, even if unsurprising — these are objective physical exam markers that can stratify risk at triage. The exclusion of RSV coinfection strengthens attribution of findings to hMPV specifically. hMPV is a major cause of acute respiratory infections in young children under 24 months of age, a group that also receives disproportionate antibiotic courses in clinical practice. Identifying which hMPV-infected children are at true hospitalization risk supports both appropriate triage and antibiotic stewardship (by framing the illness in a viral context where antibiotics are not routinely indicated).

  • Limitations:

- Generalizability threat (moderate): Two post-pandemic German winter seasons may not reflect typical hMPV epidemiology; the UK/North American disease spectrum may differ.
- RSV exclusion design (minor): While strengthening internal validity, this excludes the common RSV+hMPV coinfection scenario.
- Retrospective comparison within a prospective cohort (moderate): Ascertainment of outpatient cases likely underestimates disease severity compared to inpatients (healthier patients captured in outpatient surveillance), inflating apparent differences.
- Absence of adjusted odds ratios in the open-access portion limits quantitative applicability.
  • Clinical Implications: Practice-informing. For the ED physician evaluating a child <24 months with hMPV on multiplex PCR: objective findings of wheezing, crackles, tachypnea, and hypoxemia at presentation identify those at high risk for hospitalization. Children without these features presenting in outpatient settings likely have mild disease and should not trigger empirical antibiotics. This extends the growing evidence base that hMPV, like RSV, is primarily a clinical triage challenge rather than a diagnostic stewardship challenge.


Protection Against Recurrent Typhoid Fever Conferred by a Prior Episode: Evidence from a Large Cohort Study in Dhaka, Bangladesh
Clinical Infectious Diseases, Published Online July 6, 2026 | https://doi.org/10.1093/cid/ciag411

  • Access: OPEN ACCESS (Oxford OA — confirmed by advance article listing)

  • Design: Large population-based cohort study embedded within the controlled vaccination trial (CRT) platform in Dhaka, Bangladesh, comparing recurrence rates among typhoid cases vs. community and facility controls.

  • Background: A longstanding assumption in typhoid pathogenesis is that natural infection provides durable protective immunity — an assumption that has informed modeling studies of typhoid conjugate vaccine (TCV) programs. If natural infection does not confer reliable protection, this would argue for broader vaccine deployment and challenge the concept of "natural boosting" limiting recurrence in endemic settings. Prior animal studies and limited human data suggested partial protection, but prospective large-scale cohort data in an endemic setting were lacking.

  • Methods: In this typhoid-endemic population comprising both children and adults, the authors assessed whether natural typhoid fever confers protection against recurrent typhoid episodes. Index cases (n=940) were compared to community-based controls (n=3,760) using CRT data from Dhaka. Thirteen recurrent episodes were identified among 940 index cases and 12 episodes among 3,760 community-based controls, yielding incidence rates of 741 and 158 per 100,000 person-years, respectively, with adjusted estimates.

  • Key Findings: In this typhoid-endemic population comprising both children and adults, no evidence was found that natural typhoid fever confers protection against recurrent typhoid episodes. Findings suggest natural infection provides limited protection against recurrence in a typhoid-endemic setting. The adjusted incidence rate ratio among prior typhoid cases vs. community controls was markedly elevated (raw rates: 741 vs. 158 per 100,000 person-years — full adjusted estimates in the paper), indicating that prior infection does not confer measurable protection and may in fact reflect persistent high-exposure risk.

  • Discussion: This is a landmark finding that challenges vaccine program modeling assumptions. If natural typhoid infection does not confer durable protection, then:

1. Vaccine programs cannot assume that high-transmission communities gain "natural immunity" that reduces vaccine necessity — TCV coverage remains critical even in populations with high prior exposure.
2. Children previously treated for typhoid who live in endemic settings remain at full risk for recurrent disease and should be prioritized for TCV.
3. This has ASP implications: antibiotic treatment of typhoid should not be expected to "prime" immunity; full vaccine course completion is essential.
The distinction between facility controls and community controls in the study is methodologically important — the lack of protection seen vs. community controls (but not facility controls) suggests possible confounding by health-seeking behavior or healthcare exposure in the facility control group.
  • Limitations:

- Most critical (moderate): The study population includes both children and adults; age-stratified protection analysis would be clinically important, as children may respond differently immunologically. Pediatric-specific data not separately reported in the abstract.
- Exposure misclassification (moderate): In a high-transmission endemic setting, "prior case" status is ascertained through facility visits — subclinical infections or untreated cases in the community controls may be misclassified, biasing toward the null.
- Generalizability (minor): Bangladesh findings may not directly translate to other endemic settings with different Typhi genotypes (e.g., H58/4.3.1 clade predominance in South Asia).
  • Clinical Implications: Practice-informing for global/travel medicine and repatriated populations. U.S. pediatric ID physicians should counsel patients returning from endemic regions: prior typhoid infection provides little or no protection against recurrence; TCV or conventional vaccination remains the standard recommendation for future travel. This finding strengthens the case for universal TCV implementation in endemic countries.

Long-Acting CAB+RPV in Adolescents — Translational Context (Not a New Publication, But Newly Actionable)

While the IMPAACT 2017/MOCHA week-96 data were formally presented at CROI in February 2026 (published in Lancet HIV, January 2026), a companion article by Chandasana et al. appears in PIDJ Vol. 45(7):603-609, July 2026. This likely presents PK/safety data from the adolescent cohort. Full title and DOI are not publicly confirmed without subscription access; we are flagging this for readers with institutional PIDJ access.

Key background: IMPAACT 2017 (MOCHA) week-96 results show long-acting Cabenuva (cabotegravir+rilpivirine) effectively maintained viral suppression in 94.4% of adolescents aged 12 to <18 years who switched from daily oral HIV treatment; additionally, >97% preferred injections over daily oral treatment. The Cabenuva label was expanded to adolescents ≥12 years and ≥35 kg in 2025.

If the Chandasana et al. 🔴 BUNDIBUGYO VIRUS CLINICAL TRIAL INITIATION — DIRECT RELEVANCE TO EVACUATED PATIENTS

WHO has recommended three candidate agents for clinical trials: remdesivir, and monoclonal antibody therapies MBP-134 and maftivimab; trials of remdesivir and MBP-134 started in DRC in July 2026. For any U.S. medical center managing a confirmed or suspected BVD case: contact CDC Emergency Operations Center (770-488-7100) and reference WHO PARTNERS trial protocol for access to investigational agents. Compassionate use pathways may exist through Merck (remdesivir) and MBP-134 manufacturer contacts.

Diagnostic note: WHO has noted that initial tests for suspected Ebola cases in DRC were negative because they were not designed to detect the rare Bundibugyo strain — standard antigen RDTs do not reliably detect Bundibugyo. Only real-time PCR with Bundibugyo-specific primers confirms diagnosis. Coordinate with CDC Emergency Laboratory Response immediately for any suspected case.

With 2,231 confirmed U.S. cases and 32 active outbreaks, IG supply for post-exposure prophylaxis in immunocompromised children and infants <6 months remains a critical inventory concern. Maintain institutional supply tracking and refer to PIDS/IDSA guidance for post-exposure IG dosing. Prioritize early MMR in eligible infants 6–11 months (off-label first dose) in outbreak-adjacent communities as discussed in prior MMWR reports (previously reviewed).
