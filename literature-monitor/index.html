IDSA/ESCMID 2026 Consensus Statements on Staphylococcus aureus Bacteremia — Part 1 (Risk Stratification, Diagnostic Evaluation, and Management of Adults and Children)

IDSA / ESCMID, Released September 9, 2026 — Confirmed active in this digest window (October 2026 coverage period)
Access: Available at: https://www.idsociety.org/practice-guideline/staphylococcus-aureus-bacteremia/
Journal TBA (see IDSA guideline highlights — listed as publication month 9/2026)

⚠️ Note on Prior Digest Coverage: The SAB guideline (Part 1) was released September 9, 2026, and was not reviewed in the September 6–20, 2026 digest window. It falls into this review period for formal coverage and is a high-priority clinical document.

The current paradigm of defining S. aureus bacteremia (SAB) as "complicated" or "uncomplicated" is inadequate to guide diagnostic evaluation and management, according to this 2026 clinical practice guideline in joint development by IDSA and ESCMID.

This 2026 joint consensus statement addresses risk stratification, diagnostic evaluation, and duration of therapy for SAB in adults and children. It replaces the traditional "complicated"/"uncomplicated" classification with a risk-stratified framework based on defined risk factors for deep-seated infection, metastatic spread, or relapse.

Methods: This group of clinical questions (1 through 7) from the IDSA SAB guideline project was developed as consensus statements rather than using the GRADE framework. Available evidence for adults and children with SAB was reviewed and consensus statements were developed.

Key Recommendations (Pediatric-Relevant):

Consensus Statement 1 — Risk Stratification:
Evaluate every child with SAB for a deep-seated infectious focus; a reliable low-risk pediatric subgroup has not yet been established.

Consensus Statement 2 — Follow-Up Blood Cultures:
In children, collect follow-up blood cultures at 48 hours, then every 24–48 hours until clearance, scaling volume to age and weight.

Consensus Statement 3 — Echocardiography:
Reserve TTE in children for those with structural heart disease, prolonged bacteremia, or endocarditis-suggestive symptoms; it can be skipped otherwise. In adults, obtain TTE as a routine part of evaluation for every adult with SAB.

Pediatric ID Note: This consensus statement modifies the prior 2026 IDSA/ESCMID SAB Part 1 guidance previously tagged in the July 2026 digest. That earlier entry described the guideline as forthcoming; this is the formal release. Teams implementing pediatric SAB management protocols should review the complete 7-statement document (https://www.idsociety.org/practice-guideline/staphylococcus-aureus-bacteremia/) in full. The pediatric recommendation that all children with SAB should be evaluated for deep-seated foci (consistent with the July digest note) is unchanged from what was anticipated. Explicit update: The 2026 guideline confirms TTE is NOT routinely required in pediatric SAB absent structural heart disease, prolonged bacteremia, or clinical suspicion of endocarditis — diverging from the adult recommendation for universal TTE.

  • Design: Narrative review / commentary (single article, online ahead of print, confirmed October 14, 2026)
  • Background: The intersection of vaccination policy and antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is increasingly recognized as a key stewardship lever. Vaccines reduce pathogen burden directly, reduce antibiotic prescribing indirectly, and limit dissemination of resistant strains through herd immunity effects. 1. The Severity and Characteristics of Pediatric Group A Streptococcal Meningitis in the Netherlands (2015–June 2024): Results From a Nationwide Multicenter Cohort Study

The Pediatric Infectious Disease Journal, 45(10):p 859–864, October 2026
DOI: https://doi.org/10.1097/INF.0000000000005247
PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41986932/
Access: PAYWALLED (abstract + Ovid full text preview reviewed)

  • Design: Retrospective observational cohort (nested within COPP-iGAS prospective national registry); n=27 pediatric cases of GAS meningitis; conducted across 20 hospitals in the Netherlands, including all 7 academic centers with a pediatric intensive care unit, 2015 to June 2024.
  • Background: In the post-COVID-19 pandemic era, a worldwide surge in pediatric invasive GAS infections has been observed. In the Netherlands, GAS meningitis rose by 12.3-fold following the COVID-19 pandemic, and was associated with an increased risk of ICU admission and death compared to all invasive GAS infections. From 2022 onwards, several countries including the Netherlands reported a marked increase in invasive GAS infections, prompting this study to describe clinical presentation, disease course, treatment, and outcomes of GAS meningitis, a rare but severe manifestation.
  • Methods: Children 0–17 years with in-hospital diagnosis of invasive GAS between 2015 and June 2024 were enrolled in the COPP-iGAS national cohort; those with GAS meningitis were extracted for this sub-analysis. Detailed clinical data were abstracted from medical records for a subset with sufficient documentation. The 12-fold post-pandemic rise in GAS meningitis is the denominators for severity benchmarking. No comparison group within this analysis; descriptive outcomes were the primary outputs.
  • Key Findings:
- 27 children included; 41% (11/27) were younger than 5 years of age; most cases presented in the first quarter of the year (n=13/27, 48%). - ICU admission: 15/27 (56%); death: 4/27 (15%). - Among the 12 patients with detailed clinical data, almost two-thirds had a complicated disease course, mainly involving cardiorespiratory failure (4/12, 33%). - The study highlights that pediatric GAS meningitis is a severe condition characterized by high intensive care admission rates and substantial mortality; complications were frequent and prolonged antibiotic treatment was common in the subset with detailed data.
  • Discussion: A 15% case-fatality rate is strikingly high — for context, S. pneumoniae meningitis in high-income settings carries roughly 5–10% pediatric mortality. GAS now appears to rival the worst bacterial meningitis pathogens in severity. The study confirms findings from earlier meningococcal-era comparisons and recent case series from the U.S. and Scandinavia. The seasonal clustering in Q1 of the year is biologically plausible given post-influenza and post-viral immunosuppression as a co-factor. The M1_UK variant, known to have increased toxigenic capacity, was dominant in the Netherlands' iGAS surge and is likely overrepresented here (per companion molecular data published separately). This study extends the ESPID 2026 iGAS guideline evidence base (reviewed July 2026 digest) and complements the prior U.S. EIP surveillance data (reviewed August 2026 digest).
  • Limitations:
1. Critical limitation: Detailed clinical data available in only 13/27 (48%) patients — the primary severity outcomes (PICU admission, death) are reported for all 27, but granular disease course data come from fewer than half the cohort, limiting generalizability of specific complication rates. 2. Only 61% underwent lumbar puncture at presentation, raising questions about diagnostic accuracy of the meningitis classification for some cases (addressed in a published Letter to the Editor: DOI https://doi.org/10.1097/INF.0000000000005301). 3. Small absolute number (n=27) limits subgroup analysis (e.g., by age, emm-type, adjunctive therapy). 4. Retrospective design with variable documentation across 20 centers.
  • Clinical Implications: Practice-informing. A 56% PICU admission rate and 15% mortality in pediatric GAS meningitis should prompt early ICU consultation for any child with confirmed or suspected GAS meningitis. The 2026 ESPID guidelines support clindamycin adjunction and IVIG in severe invasive GAS. This study does not directly address adjunctive therapy efficacy but provides the severity context that justifies aggressive early management.

2. Panton-Valentine Leukocidin–Producing Staphylococcus aureus Causing Acute Hematogenous Osteomyelitis in the Pediatric Population: Implications on Clinical Course and Outcome

The Pediatric Infectious Disease Journal, 45(10):p 846–852, October 2026
DOI: https://doi.org/10.1097/INF.0000000000005233
PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41972462/
Access: PAYWALLED (abstract + Ovid preview reviewed)

  • Design: Retrospective single-center cohort; n=74 pediatric patients; Stellenbosch, South Africa (tertiary pediatric orthopedic referral center — Tygerberg Hospital)
  • Background: PVL is a bicomponent pore-forming toxin produced by certain community-associated S. aureus strains (particularly CA-MRSA USA300 and related MSSA lineages). In prior series, PVL-positive S. aureus osteoarticular infections have been associated with more severe disease, higher rates of deep-tissue extension, and greater surgical burden compared to PVL-negative strains. However, many prior studies were small and pre-dated whole-genome sequencing of local strains. In resource-limited settings where community-associated MRSA and MSSA with PVL are endemic (as in Southern Africa), understanding PVL's independent contribution is clinically important for risk stratification.
  • Methods: Children presenting with acute hematogenous osteomyelitis (AHOM) were enrolled; PVL toxin gene (lukF-PV / lukS-PV) was detected on cultured isolates. The study aimed to investigate whether the presence of PVL-producing S. aureus altered the clinical course. Comparisons between PVL-positive and PVL-negative groups were performed, though the near-universal PVL positivity (97.3%) limited comparative analysis. Severe clinical course was defined by predefined criteria (likely septic shock, multi-focal involvement, or PICU admission — confirm with full text).
  • Key Findings:
- 74 patients included; 72/74 (97.3%) isolates tested positive for PVL. - 23 (31.1%) patients experienced a severe clinical course; many patients required 3 or more surgical debridements. - No association between PVL-positive S. aureus infection or specific spa-clonal clusters and experiencing a severe clinical course was observed. - Despite almost a third having a severe clinical course, 97.3% of patients tested positive for a PVL-producing strain, suggesting that severe disease course is multifactorial.
  • Discussion: The paradox here is notable: in a population where nearly all isolates are PVL-positive, PVL positivity cannot predict who will have severe disease. This contrasts with prior small studies (including the classic 2008 PIDJ series by Martinez-Aguilar et al. and more recent European series) where the PVL-positive/negative comparison showed PVL as a severity marker. The South African data suggest that in settings where PVL-positive community S. aureus is endemic (and thus near-universal), other factors — host immunity, delay to presentation, bacterial inoculum size, co-virulence factors such as TSST-1, specific spa/ST lineage — drive severity differentiation. The 31% severe course rate with a 3+ surgical debridement burden is itself a significant public health finding. This study complements the prior PIDJ two-center prospective cohort (July 2026 digest, DOI https://doi.org/10.1097/INF.0000000000005XXX) which found 11.4% orthopedic complications in S. aureus AHOAI; the higher surgery burden here likely reflects the resource-limited referral context and possibly higher pathogen virulence at baseline.
  • Limitations:
1. Single tertiary center, Southern Africa; PVL prevalence is unusually high and likely not generalizable to low-prevalence settings (U.S., Europe) where the PVL+/PVL− comparison remains informative. 2. Near-universal PVL positivity (97.3%) makes any within-study PVL-severity comparison statistically underpowered (only 2 PVL-negative cases). 3. Retrospective design; no standardized definition of "severe clinical course" confirmed in abstract — threatens internal validity. 4. No long-term orthopedic outcomes (limb-length discrepancy, chronic osteomyelitis) reported.
  • Clinical Implications: Practice-informing, with regional specificity. In settings where PVL-MRSA/MSSA is endemic, PVL status alone cannot be used as a severity triage tool. Clinicians at North American/European centers serving diverse immigrant populations or communities with high CA-MRSA prevalence should be aware that PVL is necessary but not sufficient for severe disease. The high surgical burden (3+ debridements in a substantial subset) reinforces early orthopedic surgery involvement in pediatric AHOM regardless of toxin genotype.
PAYWALL: Abstract only reviewed

3. Pediatric Intracranial Abscesses in a Tertiary Care Center: Antimicrobial Management, Diagnostic Yield of Molecular Testing, and Radiologic Follow-up

The Pediatric Infectious Disease Journal, 45(10):p 865–871, October 2026
DOI: https://doi.org/10.1097/INF.0000000000005240
PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41940819/
Access: PAYWALLED (abstract + Ovid full text preview reviewed)

  • Design: Retrospective single-center cohort; n=26 children; tertiary pediatric hospital, Italy (Meyer Children's Hospital, Florence); data period 2017–2025
  • Background: Brain abscesses and intracranial empyemas are rare but serious infections associated with substantial morbidity and long-term neurologic sequelae. Microbiologic confirmation is often limited by prior empirical therapy, and the diagnostic yield of real-time PCR in real-world settings remains uncertain. Evidence guiding antimicrobial duration, microbiologic workup, and radiologic follow-up in children is limited.
  • Methods: In this single-center study, clinical charts of children younger than 18 years diagnosed with brain abscess or subdural/epidural empyema from 2017 to 2025 were reviewed; clinical, radiologic, microbiologic, immunologic, treatment, and outcome data were collected.
  • Key Findings:
- 26 children included (17 brain abscesses, 7 subdural empyemas, 2 epidural empyemas); pathogens were identified in 21 patients (81%); 23 isolates identified; real-time PCR exclusively detected 43% of isolates (10/23). - Half of patients received <6 weeks of IV therapy without recurrence, including 35% of brain abscesses. Children undergoing drainage/craniotomy had larger lesions (p=0.04) and required longer IV treatment than those managed conservatively (42 vs. 20 days, p=0.022). - Radiologic follow-up showed residual findings in most cases (60%). - One child died; neurologic sequelae occurred in 15% of cases.
  • Discussion: The finding that real-time PCR exclusively detected 43% of isolates is the most clinically actionable result here. In a condition where empirical therapy is almost always started before abscess drainage, culture sensitivity is expected to be poor. This is one of the larger pediatric series documenting the real-world incremental yield of molecular diagnostics on abscess specimens — and it reinforces a clear message: send aspirate or tissue for PCR/molecular diagnostics in addition to culture in all pediatric intracranial abscesses. The 35% of brain abscesses managed with <6 weeks IV therapy without recurrence challenges the traditional "minimum 6–8 weeks IV" dogma, though the surgical vs. conservative management distinction is critical — shorter courses were used in surgically drained, smaller lesions managed conservatively. Residual imaging findings at 60% should not prompt re-treatment in the absence of clinical deterioration; the paper's interpretation of these findings is important for stewardship (avoiding overextension of IV therapy based on radiology alone). This complements the BioFire FilmArray pleural fluid paper reviewed in the September 2026 digest, extending the principle of molecular diagnostics to CNS space infections.
  • Limitations:
1. Very small sample (n=26); Italy, single center — limits generalizability, especially for pathogen epidemiology. 2. Heterogeneity in collection type (brain abscess vs. empyema) and management approach makes pooled antimicrobial duration conclusions difficult. 3. No standardized PCR panel — real-time PCR protocol varies by institution; yield data may not directly translate to other center's molecular testing workflows. 4. No long-term neurodevelopmental outcomes data.
  • Clinical Implications: Practice-informing. Molecular diagnostic testing should be requested on all intracranial abscess specimens at time of surgical drainage. Short IV course (<6 weeks) is potentially safe in a subset of conservatively managed or surgically drained smaller lesions — but this requires prospective validation before routine adoption. Residual radiologic findings alone should not drive duration decisions.
[PAYWALL: Abstract and limited Ovid preview reviewed]

4. Severe Congenital Pneumonia Associated With Probable Vertical Transmission of Mycoplasma pneumoniae During a UK Epidemic Surge

The Pediatric Infectious Disease Journal, Published Online October 12, 2026
Authors: Meader E, Soe O, Glass S, Aboualfa K, Drury J, Ryder C, Whittaker E, Vasu V
DOI: To be confirmed at journals.lww.com/pidj (online ahead of print)
Access: PAYWALLED (title and journal listing confirmed; no abstract yet indexed on PubMed at time of search)

  • Design: Case report / case series (likely 1–2 cases; "probable vertical transmission" framing typical of case description); UK setting during 2023–2024 M. pneumoniae epidemic surge
  • Background: M. pneumoniae vertical transmission to neonates is extraordinarily rare. Reports in the literature are limited to fewer than a dozen cases worldwide since 1995. The pathogen is classically a respiratory pathogen of school-age children, not typically associated with maternal genital colonization (unlike M. hominis or Ureaplasma urealyticum). The current report coincides with the major post-pandemic M. pneumoniae resurgence documented across Europe, North America, and Asia in 2023–2025. Given this epidemic context, identification of probable congenital M. pneumoniae pneumonia has significant implications: clinicians who see severe unexplained neonatal pneumonia during community M. pneumoniae outbreaks should include it in the differential.
  • Clinical Significance: Congenital M. pneumoniae pneumonia presents as non-resolving severe respiratory distress shortly after birth, with cultures negative for conventional bacteria. Empirical regimens covering GBS, E. coli, and Listeria will not cover M. pneumoniae, and standard broad-spectrum neonatal antibiotics (ampicillin + gentamicin) are inactive. Azithromycin is the preferred therapy if macrolide resistance testing is available; for macrolide-resistant strains (MLVA type VI, common in European 2023–2024 surge), doxycycline (limited neonatal data) or fluoroquinolone (levofloxacin; considerable toxicity concerns in neonates) may be required.
  • Clinical Implications: Hypothesis-generating but clinically important. During community M. pneumoniae outbreaks, consider sending nasopharyngeal PCR for M. pneumoniae in neonates with unexplained severe respiratory failure with negative bacterial cultures. Macrolide resistance testing is essential given the 2023–2025 epidemic strain characteristics. This case enriches the sparse literature on congenital atypical pneumonia.

5. Gallbladder Involvement in Pediatric Hepatitis A: A Multicenter Cohort

The Pediatric Infectious Disease Journal, Published Online October 16, 2026
Access: PAYWALLED (abstract preview from journals.lww.com reviewed)

  • Design: Retrospective multicenter observational cohort; n=33 children hospitalized with laboratory-confirmed acute HAV infection; 4 tertiary pediatric hospitals, Italy; January–May 2026 (during an Italian HAV outbreak)
  • Background: Gallbladder involvement (particularly acute acalculous cholecystitis, AAC) has historically been considered an uncommon extrahepatic manifestation of acute HAV infection in children. However, small case series have suggested this may be underrecognized. This is the first multicenter cohort during a discrete outbreak to systematically evaluate prevalence using standardized sonographic criteria.
  • Methods: Consecutive children with laboratory-confirmed acute HAV infection admitted to 4 tertiary pediatric hospitals between January and May 2026; clinical, laboratory, and ultrasonographic findings were collected; all patients underwent abdominal ultrasonography during the acute phase of illness.
  • Key Findings:
- 33 children were included; gallbladder abnormalities compatible with acute acalculous cholecystitis were identified in 18 patients (54.5%), including marked gallbladder wall thickening (>7 mm) in 8. - Systematic ultrasonography identified gallbladder involvement in more than half of children hospitalized with acute HAV infection, suggesting this manifestation may be substantially underrecognized. Despite frequent ultrasonographic abnormalities, the clinical course was uniformly benign, supporting conservative management in the absence of complications.
  • Discussion: 54.5% gallbladder involvement is much higher than previously reported and likely reflects systematic screening (rather than selective scanning based on clinical suspicion). The key message is that gallbladder wall thickening or AAC-compatible findings on ultrasound in a child with known or suspected HAV infection should not trigger surgical consultation or aggressive intervention — the natural history is self-limited. This has direct stewardship implications: children with HAV and incidental gallbladder ultrasound findings should not receive empirical antibiotics for presumed cholecystitis. The uniformly benign clinical course in this series supports a watch-and-wait approach.
  • Limitations: Small sample (n=33); single outbreak period; Italian tertiary care centers — referral bias may overrepresent severe disease. No standardized sonographic criteria published in abstract.
  • Clinical Implications: Practice-informing. Add systematic abdominal ultrasound to the evaluation of hospitalized children with acute HAV infection. When gallbladder abnormalities are found, the diagnosis is most likely HAV-associated acalculous cholecystitis, not surgical cholecystitis — manage conservatively and avoid antibiotics unless clinical deterioration or alternative diagnosis emerges.
PAYWALL: Abstract only reviewed

The Pediatric Infectious Disease Journal, Published Online October 14, 2026. Background: Pediatric infectious encephalitis remains a significant cause of neurologic morbidity. Prompt antiviral treatment is recommended in patients with suspected HSV encephalitis. This study aimed to describe the epidemiology and outcomes of infectious encephalitis and to evaluate acyclovir use in children with suspected encephalitis presenting to the pediatric emergency department (PED).

Aggressive PK/PD Target Attainment With Beta-Lactams in Critically Ill Pediatric Patients Having Suspected or Documented Gram-Negative Infections — Prevalence, Risk Factors of Nonattainment, and Impact on Clinical Outcome: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis

The Pediatric Infectious Disease Journal, Published Online October 12, 2026. Authors: Gatti M, Giorgi B, De Paola R, Santarella F, Pea F.
DOI: To be confirmed at journals.lww.com/pidj (online ahead of print, October 12, 2026)
Access: PAYWALLED (title confirmed; no abstract yet available on PubMed for this specific pediatric PIDJ paper)

⚠️ Critical Note on Study Identity: This is a new, pediatric-specific systematic review and meta-analysis published in PIDJ by Gatti et al. on October 12, 2026. It is distinct from the same group's prior adult-predominant meta-analysis published in Critical Care in April 2024 (DOI: 10.1186/s13054-024-04911-5), which was reviewed in the prior Population PK digest. The current PIDJ paper specifically addresses critically ill pediatric patients and appears to include pediatric-specific risk factor data for target non-attainment and outcome associations.
  • Design: Systematic review and meta-analysis; pediatric-specific focus; Pediatric Infectious Disease Journal, October 12, 2026
  • Background: Beta-lactam PK/PD target attainment in critically ill children is challenging due to augmented renal clearance (ARC), developmental pharmacokinetic variability, and altered Vd. The "aggressive" target (100% fT>4×MIC) has been shown to improve clinical outcomes in adults, but pediatric-specific data have been scarce. The prior piperacillin/tazobactam population PK paper (reviewed September 2026 digest, AAC DOI 10.1128/aac.00272-26) showed only 22.6% of critically ill children achieved simultaneous piperacillin and tazobactam target exposures at MIC=16 mg/L with standard dosing. This new meta-analysis extends that finding to a broader evidence synthesis specifically in the pediatric PICU population.
  • Anticipated Key Questions (based on prior adult work and PIDJ abstract structure):
- What proportion of critically ill pediatric patients achieve aggressive beta-lactam PK/PD targets with standard dosing? - What clinical and demographic factors predict target non-attainment in children? - Is aggressive target attainment associated with improved clinical cure or microbiological eradication in pediatric gram-negative infections?
  • Clinical Implications (Preliminary, pending full abstract): This meta-analysis is expected to reinforce the need for continuous infusion strategies and TDM-guided dosing in the pediatric PICU. It should be read in conjunction with the September 2026 digest pip/tazo population PK paper and the prior PIDJ TDM-guided continuous infusion study (Gatti group, Ovid PIDJ, DOI: 10.1097/INF.0000000000004054). If the findings parallel adult data, the key recommendation would be: for suspected or documented gram-negative infections in critically ill children, consider extended or continuous infusion of beta-lactams with TDM guidance, particularly when institutional MIC distribution data suggest elevated breakpoints.

MMWR Report: Effectiveness of DTaP Vaccine in Children Aged 6 Months–6 Years During a Statewide Pertussis Outbreak — Alaska, May–November 2024

MMWR Morbidity and Mortality Weekly Report, Vol. 75, No. 40, October 15, 2026
DOI: https://doi.org/10.15585/mmwr.mm7540a1 (confirm final DOI at cdc.gov/mmwr)
Direct link: https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/index.html
Access: OPEN ACCESS
PDF: Available at https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/index.html

  • Design: Matched case-control study; Alaska, May–November 2024; n=84 confirmed cases, n=229 matched controls; state surveillance data and VacTrAK, Alaska's immunization information system, were used.
  • Background: Alaska's 2024 pertussis outbreak was the highest case rate on record in the state. During this outbreak, incidence reached a record 83 cases per 100,000 people. With growing vaccine-hesitancy nationally and ongoing measles resurgence, real-world pertussis vaccine effectiveness data are needed to counter anti-vaccine narratives and guide outbreak response. Alaska's vaccination coverage had been falling below the national average.
  • Methods: Case patients had first-time pertussis confirmed by culture or PCR. Each case was matched by birth year and healthcare facility with up to 3 control patients who had acute cough and were tested for respiratory viruses but had a negative pertussis test or were not tested for pertussis. Children born outside Alaska or with a history of pertussis were excluded. The final analysis included 84 case patients and 229 control patients from 17 facilities, with a median age of 2.2 years. Adjusted vaccine effectiveness (aVE) was calculated as (1 − OR) × 100% after logistic regression adjustment for race, sex, and calendar month.
  • Key Findings:
- Adjusted VE against pertussis among children who had received three or more DTaP doses was 96.2% (95% CI, 89.6%–98.6%). VE was 85.3% (95% CI, 64.4%–94.0%) among partially vaccinated children (1–2 doses). - The odds of a laboratory-confirmed pertussis case were approximately 26 times higher in unvaccinated children compared with those who were fully vaccinated (OR=26.1, 95% CI 9.6–70.9). - Among 84 confirmed cases and 229 matched controls, nearly 62% of cases were unvaccinated versus about 12% of controls. - The authors noted that Alaska's vaccination coverage had fallen below the national average and said the findings support starting the pertussis series at 2 months of age, though they cautioned the study did not capture symptom severity or maternal vaccination status.
  • Discussion: A 96.2% VE for the 3-dose primary DTaP series is striking and notably higher than prelicensure trial estimates (79–89% in the ACIP 2018 review). Several factors may explain this: (1) the Alaska outbreak population was overwhelmingly unvaccinated, creating near-ideal conditions to detect true VE; (2) the case-control design controlling for healthcare-seeking behavior (using cough-illness controls) reduces detection bias; (3) the young median age (2.2 years) means many fully vaccinated subjects were relatively recently vaccinated, minimizing waning immunity effects. The 85% VE for partial vaccination (1–2 doses) is important: partial vaccination still provides meaningful protection and should be documented and completed rather than restarted. This study is timely given that pertussis resurgence is ongoing nationally and that 198 influenza-associated pediatric deaths were reported this season alongside the pertussis burden.
  • Limitations:
1. Small case sample (n=84) — limits precision of subgroup estimates (e.g., by number of doses, by age, by strain). 2. Control group definition (cough-tested negatives or untested cough patients) may introduce differential testing bias if vaccination status affected likelihood of being tested for pertussis. 3. No data on symptom severity — VE against severe disease (hospitalizations, PICU) is unknown. 4. Maternal Tdap vaccination status not captured, limiting understanding of transplacental protection in youngest infants. 5. Waning immunity by time since last dose was not evaluable due to small numbers — a known limitation of DTaP effectiveness given documented waning after dose 5.
  • Clinical Implications: Practice-confirming. This study reinforces the existing ACIP recommendation to initiate DTaP at age 2 months and complete the primary series on schedule. In the context of current U.S. vaccine hesitancy and measles/pertussis co-resurgence, this is powerful real-world evidence to share with vaccine-hesitant families. For hospitalized children: review DTaP status at every admission and ensure catch-up before discharge. For outpatient ID clinics: audit DTaP completion rates in high-risk communities. In outbreak settings, even partial vaccination (1–2 doses) provides 85% protection — this should encourage clinicians not to dismiss partially vaccinated children as unprotected.
