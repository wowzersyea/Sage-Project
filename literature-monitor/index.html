🚨 Practice-Changing / Action Required

IDSA and ESCMID have published Part 1 of a landmark joint guideline on Staphylococcus aureus bacteremia (SAB) in adults and children, covering seven consensus statements focused on risk stratification, diagnostic evaluation (follow-up blood cultures, echocardiography, [¹⁸F]FDG-PET/CT), and duration of therapy. Part 2 (MRSA/MSSA-specific treatment) is forthcoming. This is the first IDSA-endorsed SAB guidance since the 2011 MRSA guidelines and replaces fragmented expert opinion on many contested management questions. The pediatric-specific statements are discussed in detail in the Guideline Updates section below.

📋 Guideline Updates

2026 IDSA/ESCMID Clinical Practice Guidelines on Staphylococcus aureus Bacteremia: Risk Stratification, Diagnostic Evaluation, and Management of Adults and Children — Part 1

IDSA & ESCMID, Published July 2026 | Full guideline hub: https://www.idsociety.org/2026-clinical-practice-guidelines-by-the-infectious-diseases-society-of-america-and-european-society-of-clinical-microbiology-and-infectious-diseases-on-staphylococcus-aureus-bacteremia-risk-stratification-diagnostic-evaluation-and-management-of-adults/

Individual consensus statement PDFs: https://www.idsociety.org/globalassets/idsa/practice-guidelines/sab/consensus-statement-7-manuscript.pdf

  • Access: OPEN ACCESS (consensus statement documents are freely available from IDSA)
  • Design: Consensus guideline (not GRADE-based; expert panel consensus methodology). Consensus statements 1–7 were developed without the GRADE methodology; instead, the panel reviewed existing literature and applied structured expert consensus. The panel included 23+ panelists: nine from IDSA, ten from ESCMID, and one each from PIDS, ESPID, SHEA, and ASHP.
  • Scope of Part 1: Seven consensus statements on (1) risk stratification framework, (2) follow-up blood cultures (adults), (3) follow-up blood cultures (pediatrics), (4) TTE (adults), (5) TEE (adults), (6) echocardiography (pediatrics), and (7) treatment duration for increased-risk SAB without deep-seated foci.
Methods: This group of clinical questions was developed as consensus statements rather than using the GRADE methodology. The panel included physicians and pharmacists with expertise in adult and pediatric infectious diseases and clinical microbiology from diverse geographic regions. The scope includes seven consensus statements focused on risk stratification, diagnostic evaluation (follow-up blood cultures, echocardiography, [¹⁸F]FDG-PET/CT), and duration of therapy.

PICO Questions & Recommendations:

Q1: [Population: Children with SAB | Intervention: Risk stratification approach | Comparator: No formal stratification | Outcome: Identification of low-risk children for de-escalated workup]
Recommendation: Data are insufficient to define a subgroup of children with SAB who are at low risk for deep-seated or metastatic foci of infection or relapsed bacteremia. Therefore, all children with SAB should be treated as potentially "increased risk" pending further prospective data.
Strength / Quality: Consensus statement (not GRADE); low-quality evidence base for pediatric risk stratification
Supporting data: No validated pediatric risk score identified in literature review; adult risk scores (e.g., PREDICT score, Fowler criteria) have not been validated in children

Q2: [Population: Pediatric patients with SAB | Intervention: Follow-up blood cultures (FUBC) | Comparator: Single clearance culture or none | Outcome: Detection of persistent bacteremia]
Recommendation: In pediatric patients with SAB, the panel suggests FUBC be obtained at 48 hours after sampling of the first positive blood culture and then repeated every 24 to 48 hours until negative to document blood culture clearance.
Strength / Quality: Consensus suggestion; extrapolated from adult data and pediatric clinical experience
Supporting data: No RCT data in children; adult data support 48-hour FUBC sensitivity for persistent bacteremia

Q3: [Population: Pediatric patients with SAB without identified deep-seated/metastatic foci | Intervention: TTE | Comparator: No echocardiography | Outcome: Detection of endocarditis]
Recommendation: The panel suggests not performing routine TTE in all pediatric patients with SAB without identified deep-seated or metastatic foci after diagnostic evaluation — a notable departure from adult recommendations, where TTE is suggested routinely.
Strength / Quality: Consensus suggestion; low-quality evidence
Supporting data: Low pediatric endocarditis prevalence in uncomplicated SAB; operator-dependent TTE quality in pediatrics; risk-benefit analysis favoring selective use

Q4: [Population: Pediatric patients with SAB without deep-seated/metastatic foci after full diagnostic evaluation | Intervention: 14-day antibiotic duration | Comparator: Shorter or longer courses | Outcome: Clinical cure, relapse]
Recommendation: In pediatric patients with SAB without deep-seated or metastatic foci of infection after diagnostic evaluation, the panel suggests antibiotic treatment for 14 days.
Strength / Quality: Consensus suggestion; moderate-quality evidence
Supporting data: Consistent with prior IDSA MRSA guidance; no pediatric RCT comparing durations; adult SABATO trial (7 vs. 14 days) excluded children

Q5 (adults): [Population: Adult patients with SAB | Intervention: Routine TTE | Comparator: TEE without TTE | Outcome: Detection of endocarditis]
Recommendation: The panel suggests routinely performing TTE in all adults with SAB, since the panel could not identify criteria to clearly define a population at very low risk of infective endocarditis.
Strength / Quality: Consensus suggestion; moderate-quality evidence
Supporting data: Landmark observational studies show ~10–25% IE prevalence in unselected SAB; TTE sensitivity for IE in adults is ~70–80%

Q6 (adults): [Population: Adult patients with SAB | Intervention: TEE after negative TTE | Comparator: TTE alone | Outcome: Detection of endocarditis]
Recommendation: The panel suggests that TEE may be unnecessary in adults with SAB who have a negative good-quality TTE and are without any endocarditis increased-risk features.
Strength / Quality: Consensus suggestion; moderate-quality evidence
Supporting data: Good-quality TTE negative predictive value for vegetations is high when TTE windows are adequate; TEE adds cost and procedural risk

Q7 (adults): [Population: Adults with "increased-risk" SAB (e.g., community onset, persistent bacteremia) without deep-seated/metastatic foci | Intervention: Extended antibiotic therapy | Comparator: Standard 14-day course | Outcome: Relapse, mortality]
Recommendation: Extended duration therapy (e.g., beyond standard 14 days) may be appropriate for increased-risk patients without evidence of deep-seated foci; exact duration individualized based on risk factors.
Strength / Quality: Consensus suggestion; moderate-quality evidence
Supporting data: Positive blood cultures obtained ≥48 hours after the first positive blood culture may be associated with the presence of deep-seated or metastatic foci of infection (e.g., osteomyelitis, endocarditis, septic thrombophlebitis).

Clinical Implications for Pediatric ID/ASP Programs:
This guideline is practice-changing for pediatric SAB management in two key areas:
1. TTE is NOT routinely recommended in all children with SAB — unlike adults. This may permit more selective echocardiography in uncomplicated, low-risk pediatric cases, reducing procedural burden and cost.
2. 14-day treatment duration is now formally consensus-supported for pediatric SAB without deep-seated foci, providing defensible backing for shorter courses in selected cases.
3. All children with SAB should receive follow-up cultures at 48h and every 24–48h until clearance — this should be codified in institutional order sets.
4. The absence of a validated pediatric risk score is highlighted as a significant gap; ID consultation for all pediatric SAB cases remains strongly implied by the guideline's complexity.

⚠️ Note: Part 2 of this guideline covering MRSA and MSSA-specific antibiotic selection and therapy optimization is still forthcoming. Continue current institutional protocols for antibiotic choice pending that publication.

💊 Stewardship Highlights

Universal VRE Screening in a High-Acuity PICU: Clinical Yield, Infection Outcomes, and Implications for Surveillance Program Design

The Pediatric Infectious Disease Journal, Published Online July 22, 2026 | https://www.ovid.com/jnls/pidj (DOI pending indexing — access via Ovid PIDJ advance online)
  • Access: PAYWALLED (advance online article, abstract available)
  • Design: Single-center, retrospective cohort study. This study evaluated universal VRE surveillance by describing the clinical characteristics and risk profiles of asymptomatic VRE-colonized patients in a newly established, high-acuity, 54-bed PICU; it included all patients with positive admission or weekly rectal VRE screening cultures between October 2023 and October 2024.
  • Background: Routine surveillance for VRE colonization remains standard practice in many ICUs; however, in high-acuity PICUs where clinical VRE infections are uncommon, universal screening may impose substantial financial and operational burdens with uncertain clinical benefits.
  • Key Findings: In this high-acuity PICU with robust infection prevention infrastructure, VRE colonization, including colonization with high-risk E. faecium strains, did not progress to clinical infection. These findings suggest that in high-acuity PICUs with established infection control excellence, the clinical yield of universal VRE screening may be marginal compared with its operational costs, supporting a transition toward targeted, risk-based surveillance.
  • Limitations: Single center; 12-month observation window; generalizability to PICUs with less-robust infection control or higher community VRE prevalence is limited. Absence of progression to infection may reflect excellent IPC rather than low inherent risk of VRE-colonized PICU patients.
  • Clinical Implications: Practice-informing for ASP and infection prevention teams reconsidering universal VRE surveillance programs. This is consistent with emerging evidence from adult ICU settings questioning the cost-effectiveness of universal screening. Programs should evaluate their local VRE infection rates, IPC infrastructure, and MDRO burden before abandoning universal protocols.

🦠 Pediatric ID Studies

1. Duration of Bacteremia and Orthopedic Outcomes in Children with S. aureus Acute Hematogenous Osteoarticular Infection (AHOAI): A Two-Center Prospective Surveillance Cohort

The Pediatric Infectious Disease Journal, Published Online July 21, 2026 | https://www.ovid.com/jnls/pidj (DOI pending indexing — access via Ovid PIDJ advance online)
  • Access: PAYWALLED
  • Design: Prospective cohort study (nested within prospective surveillance). Children ≤18 years old with AHOAI secondary to S. aureus from 2011 to 2021 were identified through prospective surveillance studies at Texas Children's Hospital and St. Louis Children's Hospital. S. aureus is the predominant cause of acute hematogenous osteoarticular infection (osteomyelitis or bacterial arthritis) in children and is commonly associated with bacteremia.
  • Background: Current guidelines suggest that persistent bacteremia may warrant more aggressive treatment, but supporting data in children are limited. The clinical significance of bacteremia duration on long-term orthopedic outcomes in pediatric AHOAI has not been well characterized in large prospective cohorts.
  • Methods: Outcomes of children with S. aureus AHOAI were evaluated with respect to bacteremia duration; orthopedic complications included pathologic fractures, avascular necrosis, angular deformity, and chronic osteomyelitis. Per prior conference abstract of the same dataset (OFID 2023), prolonged bacteremia was defined a priori as the upper quartile of duration, n=532 total cases, 343 with ≥1 positive blood culture; primary outcome was orthopedic complications.
  • Key Findings (based on abstract and prior preliminary data): Overall, 532 cases were identified, of which 343 (64.5%) had ≥1 positive blood culture; 61 developed orthopedic complications (11.4%). The median duration of bacteremia was 1 day (IQR: 1–3 days). This is the most comprehensive published pediatric dataset on this question from high-volume centers with prospective capture.
  • Limitations: Two academic tertiary centers limit generalizability; classification of bacteremia duration relies on number of positive cultures rather than continuous measurement; unmeasured confounders (imaging findings, surgical timing) may affect outcomes.
  • Clinical Implications: This dataset directly informs the clinical dilemma of how aggressively to escalate therapy in children with prolonged bacteremia in AHOAI — a question highlighted but not resolved by the new IDSA/ESCMID SAB guidelines. Practice-informing for inpatient pediatric orthopedic ID teams.

2. Molecular Epidemiology and Genotypic Shift of Coxsackievirus A16 and Co-circulating Enteroviruses in Pediatric Hand, Foot, and Mouth Disease — Multicenter Surveillance, China

The Pediatric Infectious Disease Journal, Published Online July 22, 2026 | https://www.ovid.com/jnls/pidj (DOI pending indexing — access via Ovid PIDJ advance online)
  • Access: PAYWALLED
  • Design: Prospective-retrospective molecular epidemiology surveillance study. Epidemiologic data and clinical specimens were collected from reported HFMD cases; pan-enterovirus, EV-A71, CVA16, CVA6, and CVA10 were detected by real-time PCR; VP1 regions of CVA6 and CVA16 were amplified, sequenced, and phylogenetically analyzed.
  • Background: HFMD is a common pediatric infectious disease that frequently causes outbreaks in the Asia-Pacific region; the causative pathogens are dynamically changing, with a notable genotypic shift observed in CVA16 across China. Following introduction of EV-A71 vaccines, CVA6 has emerged as the dominant HFMD pathogen in many regions, with CVA16 continuing to co-circulate. Understanding real-time genotypic dynamics is critical for vaccine development targeting post-EV-A71 era pathogens.
  • Key Findings: Among 2,596 specimens, 2,148 (82.7%) were enterovirus-positive; proportions were: CVA6 52.8%, CVA16 23.8%, CVA10 5.0%, EV-A71 0.3%. CVA6 predominated in 2020, 2022, and 2023; CVA16 predominated in 2021 and 2024. Phylogenetic analysis of CVA16 VP1 sequences demonstrated emergence of a new subgenotype (B1c) supplanting previously dominant B1a/B1b lineages.
  • Limitations: China-specific data; limited clinical severity outcomes reported; enterovirus testing was specimen-type dependent; phylogenetic conclusions require corroboration with global surveillance.
  • Clinical Implications: Practice-informing for U.S. pediatric ID clinicians counseling families on HFMD outbreaks: CVA6 remains dominant and causes more atypical/severe presentations (including eczema herpeticum-like rash, onychomadesis) than classic CVA16. The CVA16 B1c genotypic shift has unknown virulence implications but warrants monitoring. EV-A71 remains negligible (<0.3%), reflecting vaccine impact in China. No available treatment or active surveillance program for these pathogens in the U.S. — supportive management is standard.

📰 Notable General ID / MMWR

Genotype-Specific Detection of Measles Virus Using Wastewater Surveillance — Wisconsin, February 2026

MMWR Morbidity and Mortality Weekly Report, Vol. 75, No. 27, July 16, 2026 | DOI: https://doi.org/10.15585/mmwr.mm7527a1 PDF: https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/75/wr/mm7527a1.htm (OPEN ACCESS)
  • Access: OPEN ACCESS
  • Design: Field investigation / public health laboratory report (descriptive case study with molecular epidemiology). Two unrelated measles cases reported in Wisconsin, February 2026, with concurrent wastewater surveillance data.
  • Background: Wastewater surveillance (WWS) has emerged as an early-warning tool for measles during the ongoing U.S. outbreak — as of July 23, 2026, 2,318 confirmed measles cases have been reported in the United States in 2026 — making surveillance system sensitivity paramount. Prior wastewater work in Colorado and Oregon demonstrated that WWS can precede clinical case reporting by days. There have been 35 new outbreaks reported in 2026, and 93% of confirmed cases (2,153 of 2,318) are outbreak-associated.
  • Methods: The CDC-validated wastewater assay for measles uses a reverse transcription droplet digital PCR (RT-ddPCR) targeting the nucleoprotein (N) gene. Wisconsin tested archived wastewater samples against two confirmed measles cases in February 2026 — one genotype D8 (dominant circulating strain in U.S. outbreaks), one genotype B3 (internationally circulating variant).
  • Key Findings — The Critical Technical Problem:
During the period after the original assay was released (May 2025–February 2026), 75% (169 of 226) of available measles B3 sequences contained a mutation in the N-gene probe-binding region that the original assay targeted. Sample collection dates suggest this variant has been the predominant B3 variant in international circulation since July 2024.

In practical terms:
- Case 1 (genotype D8): Wastewater detection preceded clinical case report — functioning as intended.
- Case 2 (genotype B3): Wastewater FAILED TO DETECT the case due to the N-gene mutation. CDC found that wastewater surveillance failed to detect an internationally circulating measles genotype that accounted for about 5% to 10% of U.S. measles cases in 2025. The second case, caused by the international B3 genotype, was undetected; public health officials later released a modified laboratory procedure that improved detection of the B3 genotype.

The modified B3 probe sequence was released in March 2026: an adenine-to-guanine (A-to-G) mutation in the probe-binding region of genotype B3 caused the mismatch; a modified probe was added in March 2026.

  • Discussion: This report identifies a fundamental limitation of sequence-targeting wastewater assays: pathogen evolution can silently degrade surveillance sensitivity. The B3 genotype failure affected systems that had not yet updated to the March 2026 modified probe. This is the molecular equivalent of influenza antigenic mismatch — a reminder that wastewater assay performance must be continuously re-validated against circulating strains. The clinical significance is that WWS-negative results in a community experiencing HFMD-like or rash illness should not be used to exclude measles circulation when the B3 genotype is plausible (e.g., recent international travel link in community).
  • Limitations: Descriptive two-case report; cannot quantify assay sensitivity systematically from n=2; the Wisconsin cases may not be representative of the national B3 prevalence. B3 accounts for a minority of current U.S. cases (5–10%), so the overall impact of the original assay failure on national surveillance sensitivity is likely modest but not trivial.
  • Clinical Implications for Pediatric ID Clinicians:
1. Do NOT use a negative wastewater result to rule out measles in a child with compatible illness in a community where international travelers circulate (B3 is more common internationally). 2. Labs using wastewater for measles surveillance should confirm their assay has been updated to the March 2026 modified B3 probe. 3. The updated case count — 2,318 as of July 23, 2026 — represents a genuine increase from 2,073 YTD reported in the prior digest. The outbreak-associated fraction (93%) means ongoing transmission chains are the dominant driver, not sporadic importations. 4. This is practice-informing for public health decisions but does not change clinical diagnostic algorithms (PCR of nasopharyngeal swab and/or urine remain the standard for clinical diagnosis).

⚠️ Measles Situational Update — July 23–26, 2026

As of July 23, 2026, 2,318 confirmed measles cases have been reported in the United States in 2026. There have been 35 new outbreaks reported in 2026, and 93% of confirmed cases (2,153 of 2,318) are outbreak-associated (782 from outbreaks starting in 2026 and 1,371 from outbreaks that started in 2025). For the full year of 2025, a total of 2,289 confirmed measles cases were reported in the United States — meaning 2026 has already surpassed the full-year 2025 count with 5+ months remaining.

Implications for pediatric practice:

  • Measles vaccination verification is now urgent at every clinical encounter. Children with documented two-dose MMR history are protected; first-dose efficacy (~93–95%) is sufficient for most exposures but insufficient in high-density outbreak settings.

  • Wastewater surveillance is now an active tool in many U.S. states but has the B3 genotype detection blind spot described above (now corrected in updated assays).

  • IG supply pressure (IGIM and IGIV) should be anticipated during ongoing outbreak periods. Contact your pharmacy team regarding institutional stock.
