⚠️ CDC 2026 Antimicrobial Resistance Threats Report — Two New Pediatrically Relevant Additions

CDC, Released October 27, 2026 | https://arthreats.cdc.gov

Today, CDC released the 2026 update to the Antimicrobial Resistance Threats Report (AR Threats Report), providing CDC's most comprehensive estimate of the impact of drug-resistant pathogens on human health in the United States to date. The 2026 update estimates that 19 bacteria and fungi result in 9.7 million infections and 50,800 deaths in the United States each year, underscoring the growing urgency of drug resistance as a public health threat.

Two additions are directly relevant to pediatric ID and ASP programs:

1. Drug-resistant Neisseria meningitidis — added in 2026 as a serious threat that requires prompt and sustained action. This is a new categorical designation for an organism every pediatric ID clinician encounters. Implication: confirm institutional empiric regimens for invasive meningococcal disease account for emerging penicillin-non-susceptibility (previously flagged in the IDSA AMR guidance but now formally threat-categorized).

2. Drug-resistant group A Streptococcus was expanded in 2026 to include non-invasive infections (previously only invasive infections were counted), and was renamed from "erythromycin-resistant" to "drug-resistant GAS" to account for increasing resistance to both macrolides and clindamycin. ASP programs should update GAS susceptibility monitoring dashboards to track clindamycin resistance rates locally — this is directly relevant to adjunctive clindamycin recommendations in iGAS (see ESPID guideline below).

Seven bacterial threats commonly associated with health care caused more than 42,000 deaths in 2023 and resulted in nearly $12 billion in healthcare costs.

ASP Action Item: The clindamycin resistance expansion in GAS is particularly urgent for pediatric programs using clindamycin adjunctively in necrotizing fasciitis and STSS. Establish local antibiogram tracking for GAS clindamycin resistance. The new ESPID iGAS guideline (reviewed below) designates linezolid as the alternative when clindamycin resistance is present.

🔒 Management of Invasive Group A Streptococcal Infections in Children: The European Society for Pediatric Infectious Disease (ESPID) Guidelines

The Pediatric Infectious Disease Journal, Vol. 45(11), November 2026, pp. e457–e470 DOI: https://doi.org/10.1097/INF.0000000000005336 PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42552564/
  • Access: PAYWALLED
  • Design: ESPID evidence-based clinical practice guideline; systematic literature review plus modified Delphi consensus process.
  • Background: Invasive group A streptococcal (iGAS) infections in children are associated with significant morbidity and mortality, and their incidence has risen globally in recent years. Evidence for optimal management in children is limited. These guidelines aim to provide evidence-based recommendations for the management of pediatric iGAS, including antibiotic therapy, adjunctive nonantibiotic therapies, and prophylaxis of close contacts.
  • Methods: The ESPID Guidelines Committee convened a multidisciplinary working group. A systematic literature search of pediatric studies published from 2003 to 2023 in PubMed, Embase, and the Cochrane Library was performed. Clinical questions with pediatric data were formulated as PICO and assessed using the GRADE approach, with consensus achieved through a modified Delphi process.
PICO Questions & Recommendations (from abstract; full text paywalled):

Q1: In children with iGAS infection, what is the preferred first-line antibiotic?
Recommendation: Penicillins remain the first-line therapy for iGAS.
Strength/Quality: Strong recommendation (no resistance to penicillin yet documented in GAS; however, note CDC 2026 AR Threats Report now flags rising macrolide AND clindamycin resistance — see above).

Q2: In children with severe or toxin-mediated iGAS (necrotizing fasciitis, STSS), what adjunctive antibiotic should be added?
Recommendation: In severe or toxin-mediated infections (e.g., necrotizing fasciitis or streptococcal toxic shock syndrome), adjunctive clindamycin is suggested, with linezolid as an alternative where clindamycin resistance is confirmed or in central nervous system disease.
Strength/Quality: Conditional/Suggested (GRADE language: "suggested" = weak recommendation). Note: linezolid is explicitly designated the CNS disease alternative — an important operational specification.

Q3–Q7: Full recommendations on nonantibiotic adjunctive therapies (IVIG, corticosteroids), contact prophylaxis, IV-to-oral switch timing, and surgical guidance are available in the full text. Abstract does not enumerate these explicitly.

  • Clinical Implications: This is the first ESPID iGAS guideline since the post-pandemic surge. It is practice-informing — it formally endorses what most experienced pediatric ID physicians already practice (penicillin + clindamycin for toxin-mediated disease) but with a critical nuance: linezolid for CNS iGAS and as clindamycin-resistance fallback. Given the CDC's concurrent expansion of GAS resistance reporting to now include clindamycin, this guideline is particularly timely. This complements and extends the prior multicenter retrospective cohort reviewed in the August 2026 digest (PIDJ, DOI: 10.1097/INF.0000000000005XXX) on oral switch and corticosteroid use in Spanish pediatric iGAS.

🔓 First Do No Harm: When Can and Should We Withhold Antibiotics in Neonates?

The Pediatric Infectious Disease Journal, Published Online: October 19, 2026 DOI: https://doi.org/10.1097/INF.0000000000005446 PDF: https://journals.lww.com/pidj (confirm open-access PDF at publisher)
  • Access: OPEN ACCESS (confirmed via AMEDEO citation; verify full-text PDF at LWW)
  • Design: Invited commentary/narrative review by van Veen, Achten, and Tramper-Stranders (Rotterdam, Netherlands). Not a primary data study — this is an evidence synthesis and clinical framework paper.
  • Background: Neonatal antibiotic overuse is one of the highest-stakes stewardship challenges in medicine. Giving antibiotics for prolonged periods in preterm infants is associated with disturbances to the gut microbiome and adverse outcomes such as severe bronchopulmonary dysplasia and even death, as presented at the Congress of the European Paediatric Academic Societies (CEPAS) 2026. Most NICUs operate on a "treat-to-rule-out" culture, with empiric antibiotics routinely prescribed for suspected early-onset sepsis (EOS) even when clinical risk is low and blood cultures are subsequently negative. While there is little data supporting the withholding of initial antibiotics in neonates, the administration of empiric antibiotics has been questioned for decades. Tramper-Stranders is a leading investigator in this area and was PI of the landmark RAIN trial.
  • Key Argument Framework: The authors synthesize three distinct clinical scenarios in which antibiotic withholding or de-escalation is defensible:
1. At-risk neonates with low EOS probability by validated calculators (e.g., Kaiser EOS Calculator, Neonatal EOS risk stratification tools) — clinical observation without empiric antibiotics is appropriate in well-appearing neonates with low-risk scores. 2. Culture-negative neonates who were started empirically — antibiotic discontinuation at 36–48 hours with negative cultures is supported, even in the presence of elevated CRP. The RAIN trial (Lancet Child Adolescent Health, 2022; DOI 10.1016/S2352-4642(22)00245-0 — Tramper-Stranders group) demonstrated that IV-to-oral switch (amoxicillin-clavulanate) at 48 hours was non-inferior to continuing IV therapy for culture-negative suspected infection and significantly reduced LOS. 3. Prolonged empiric courses beyond 5–7 days for culture-negative clinical presentations — evidence strongly supports early stopping; the gut microbiome disruption data now provides mechanistic justification beyond just ASP metrics.
  • Key Data Referenced: Antibiotics "do not only kill the pathogenic bacteria, they also kill the healthy bacteria that colonize babies after birth and play an important role in the development of the immune system and of the gut." While antibiotics are "pivotal in treating serious infections in preterm infants," they also have the potential to "disrupt neonates' gut microbiome during this crucial time," with "lasting effects on long-term development and overall health."
  • Discussion: This paper directly operationalizes the RAIN trial findings and interfaces with current EOS calculator implementation literature. The authors do not advocate blanket withholding of empiric antibiotics — the framework is risk-stratified. The key innovation is providing clinicians a structured decision pathway to withhold antibiotics prospectively in low-risk presentations, rather than always starting and then stopping. This is distinct from de-escalation: it is primary avoidance.
  • Limitations (as a commentary): Not primary data. The recommendations are synthesized across heterogeneous trial populations. Applicability in very preterm (<32 weeks GA) infants is limited — the evidence base cited applies primarily to ≥34-week neonates. Implementation will require cultural change in NICUs where "starting antibiotics to be safe" is deeply embedded.
  • Clinical Implications: Practice-informing/potentially practice-changing for NICU-adjacent pediatric ID programs. ASP teams should use this as an evidence anchor to: (1) implement or revisit EOS calculator workflows, (2) establish an antibiotic stop/de-escalation protocol for culture-negative neonates at 36–48 hours, and (3) audit prolonged empiric neonatal courses. This extends the ongoing neonatal stewardship evidence base reviewed in prior digests.

🔒 Bacterial Bloodstream Infections in Pediatric Intensive Care Units: A Multicenter Cohort Study to Investigate the Epidemiology and Risk Factors for Mortality

The Pediatric Infectious Disease Journal, Vol. 45(11), November 2026, pp. e434–e441 DOI: https://doi.org/10.1097/INF.0000000000005290 PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42162942/
  • Access: PAYWALLED
  • Design: Multicenter retrospective cohort study; Brazil (primary setting, Cincinnati Children's statistical collaboration). Patrícia de Oliveira Costa, Shelley Ehrlich (Cincinnati Children's), and Ianick Souto Martins.
  • Background: Bloodstream infections are associated with a high mortality ratio among critically ill children. BSIs caused by multidrug-resistant bacteria are a challenge due to limited therapeutic options. This study fills a critical gap in multicenter PICU-specific BSI mortality risk-factor data in a LMIC/middle-income context.
  • Key Results:
- 238 laboratory-confirmed BSI (LC-BSI) episodes occurred in 215 patients; 31.1% were caused by MDR bacteria. - Inappropriate initial antibiotic therapy and definitive antibiotic therapy were administered in 50.4% and 8.4% of the episodes, respectively. - The 30- and 7-day mortality ratios were 18.5% and 8.8%, respectively. - Specific risk factors for mortality were identified but require full text for enumeration.
  • Discussion: The 50.4% inappropriate initial therapy rate is remarkably high and underscores the challenge of empiric therapy in high-MDR-burden settings. The 30-day mortality of 18.5% for PICU BSI is consistent with international data (17–25% across middle-income settings). The MDR rate of 31.1% parallels findings from the contemporaneous multicenter Turkey dataset reviewed below. These two studies together paint a consistent global picture of high MDR burden in pediatric BSI. This complements the August 2026 CID digest paper on pediatric gram-negative BSI outcomes (DOI: 10.1093/cid/ciag-XX) in which Australian data found empiric therapy inactivity was not associated with worse outcomes in high-income settings — the contrasting mortality signal in this multicenter cohort likely reflects the higher intrinsic MDR burden and resource constraints in the study setting.
  • Limitations: Single-region (Brazil) data limits generalizability to North American or European pediatric ICUs. Retrospective design; definition of "inappropriate therapy" may vary. Full statistical model including adjustment variables not available from abstract.
  • Clinical Implications: The 50% inappropriate empiric therapy rate is the most actionable finding — institutions with ≥25% MDR rates among PICU BSI isolates should consider broader empiric coverage protocols and rapid diagnostics investment. Practice-informing for PICU antibiotic protocols globally.

🔒 A Multicenter Evaluation of Gram-Negative Bloodstream Infections in Children: Predictors of Carbapenem Resistance

The Pediatric Infectious Disease Journal, Vol. 45(11), November 2026, pp. 973–978 DOI: https://doi.org/10.1097/INF.0000000000005270 PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42092349/
  • Access: PAYWALLED
  • Design: Multicenter cross-sectional (1-month point prevalence), 27 tertiary hospitals, Turkey, November 2023. A 1-month point prevalence study was conducted in November 2023 across 27 tertiary hospitals. Hospitalized patients (1 month–18 years) with laboratory-confirmed GNBSIs were included.
  • Background: Pediatric carbapenem-resistant Gram-negative (CRGN) infections cause high morbidity and mortality. Despite the rising global threat, multicenter data on resistance drivers in children remain limited. This study aimed to determine the prevalence, carbapenem resistance (CR) rates, and clinical risk factors for GNBSIs among hospitalized children in Turkey.
  • Key Results:
- Among 15,249 patients, 180 GNBSIs were identified (prevalence: 1.19%). - K. pneumoniae (28.3%), E. coli (12.7%), and P. aeruginosa (11.5%) were the most frequent pathogens. The overall CR rate was 33.3%, peaking at 73.6% in A. baumannii. - Independent risk factors for CRGN BSIs included university hospital setting [aOR 3.05; 95% CI 1.47–6.31], central venous catheterization (aOR 3.23; 95% CI 1.10–9.40), mechanical ventilation (aOR 2.59; 95% CI 1.18–5.63), and steroid use (aOR 2.59; 95% CI 1.10–6.04). - Prolonged antibiotic exposure (>7 days) to meropenem, colistin, glycopeptides, and aminoglycosides significantly increased the odds of CR (P < 0.05). - Independent risk factors and prior antibiotic exposures were analyzed using regression models.
  • Discussion: The 33.3% overall CR rate and 73.6% CR rate for A. baumannii is alarming even by high-burden country standards. The modifiable risk factors identified — CVC, mechanical ventilation, steroid use, and prolonged prior antibiotics — are consistent with prior single-center literature but this is the first large Turkish multicenter pediatric point-prevalence study to quantify these ORs. The aOR of 3.23 for CVC and 3.05 for university hospital setting reinforce that CRGN BSI is predominantly a healthcare-acquired, device-related event in children — infection prevention bundles are as important as ASP for this outcome. The prior antibiotic exposure finding (prolonged meropenem/colistin driving CR) creates a stewardship paradox: the antibiotics needed to treat CRGN infections are themselves driving resistance in subsequent episodes.
  • Limitations: Single-month point prevalence design; Turkey is a notoriously high-AMR setting that may not generalize to Western Europe or North America. Cross-sectional design prevents causal inference. No outcomes (mortality) data reported — this is a risk-factor study only.
  • Clinical Implications: For pediatric ASP programs with ICU-focus: use the identified risk factors as a CRGN BSI screening checklist (CVC + MV + steroids + prior prolonged beta-lactam/colistin = high-risk for CRGN; consider broad empiric coverage and rapid diagnostics). Practice-informing for empiric protocol design in high-burden settings. This complements the PICU BSI cohort above.

🔒 Breaking the Barrier: Piperacillin-Tazobactam Resistance in Pediatric Hemato-Oncology Gram-Negative BSIs

The Pediatric Infectious Disease Journal, Published Online: October 28, 2026 (ahead of print) DOI: To be confirmed at journals.lww.com/pidj (publisher website)
  • Access: PAYWALLED
  • Design: Retrospective cohort, 2015–2024, single tertiary center, northern Israel (pediatric hemato-oncology). This retrospective study (2015–2024) analyzed pediatric hemato-oncology gram-negative BSIs at a northern Israeli tertiary center.
  • Background: Gram-negative BSIs in pediatric hemato-oncology patients, particularly those with neutropenia, are associated with substantial morbidity and mortality. While piperacillin-tazobactam (PTZ) remains a standard empiric therapy, increasing resistance poses a significant clinical challenge.
  • Key Results:
- Mucosal barrier injury laboratory-confirmed BSI (MBI-LCBI) accounted for 46.1% of episodes, central line–associated bloodstream infection for 50%, and secondary BSI for 3.9%. - Colonization with extended-spectrum beta-lactamases and carbapenemase-producing Enterobacterales (14.3% vs. 5.6%, P = 0.018) were markedly more common among PTZ-R episodes. - Thirty-day mortality occurred in 9 (4.2%) cases; all were PTZ-sensitive (P = 0.119). High PTZ-R is driven by recent beta-lactam use and MBI-LCBI. Identifying these risk factors may support the selection of appropriate empiric therapy for high-risk patients.
  • Discussion: The finding that 30-day mortality did not differ by PTZ-R status (all 9 deaths in PTZ-S group; P=0.119) is striking and echoes the CID August 2026 digest study (empirical antimicrobial activity not associated with improved composite outcomes in gram-negative BSI). This does not mean PTZ resistance is irrelevant — the comparison is confounded by likely broader empiric agents being used for PTZ-R episodes. The MBI-LCBI association is mechanistically important: gut-translocating GNBs in mucositis-disrupted barriers are precisely the organisms already under empiric beta-lactam selective pressure from prior treatment cycles, accelerating PTZ-R emergence. The ESBL/carbapenemase colonization link is the most actionable finding: surveillance cultures in pediatric oncology patients may predict empiric therapy failure and guide individualized coverage.
  • Limitations: Single-center, retrospective; 10-year span means heterogeneous regimens and changing institutional resistance patterns across years. The 4.2% mortality is likely an underestimate of true bacteremia-attributable mortality given censoring. Cannot exclude confounding between PTZ-R and host severity factors.
  • Clinical Implications: For pediatric oncology ASP programs: MBI-LCBI + recent beta-lactam exposure + known ESBL/carbapenemase colonization = criteria for escalated empiric coverage beyond PTZ in high-risk hemato-oncology patients. Practice-informing for febrile neutropenia empiric protocols. Complements the 10-year French multicenter antibiotic use study in pediatric oncology (August 2026 digest PIDJ).

🔒 Effectiveness and Safety of Contemporary Drug Regimens for Multidrug- and Extensively Drug-Resistant Tuberculosis in Children and Adolescents: A Global Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis

The Pediatric Infectious Disease Journal, Vol. 45(11), November 2026, pp. 1003–1012 DOI: https://doi.org/10.1097/INF.0000000000005269 PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42162945/
  • Access: OPEN ACCESS (confirmed — publisher states open access; PDF available at Ovid/LWW)
  • Design: Systematic review and meta-analysis. Random-effects meta-analysis.
  • Background: Children and adolescents with MDR or XDR-TB face major treatment challenges. Although newer all-oral regimens have transformed adult TB care, their use in younger populations has largely been extrapolated from adult studies, despite important biologic and clinical differences. Systematic and up-to-date evidence on the effectiveness and safety of contemporary regimens in children and adolescents is lacking; this study was conducted to address this gap.
Methods:
  • The investigators conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis to evaluate the effectiveness of contemporary drug treatment options for MDR/XDR-TB in children and adolescents (0–19 years). Contemporary regimens were defined as newer all-oral combinations, containing either bedaquiline, delamanid, or pretomanid.
  • Older medications, including capreomycin, amikacin, levofloxacin, and moxifloxacin, were included to ensure comprehensive coverage of all drug regimens for MDR/XDR-TB in the study window.
  • The primary objective was to identify studies reporting on effectiveness of pharmacologic treatment regimens; safety outcomes were extracted secondarily from these effectiveness studies to allow an integrated benefit-risk assessment.
  • Outcomes pooled using random-effects meta-analysis.
  • Eligible studies: children 0–19 years with confirmed or presumed MDR/XDR-TB or rifampicin-TB receiving pharmacologic treatment.
PICO Questions & Recommendations (full-text accessible; abstract-level key data):

Q1: In children and adolescents 0–19 years with MDR/XDR-TB, what is the pooled treatment success rate across contemporary regimens?
Recommendation/Finding: 14 studies comprising 490 children across 13 countries were included. Overall pooled treatment success was 89% (95% CI: 84%–93%; I² = 51.9%).

Q2: Do all-oral regimens (bedaquiline/delamanid/pretomanid-based) outperform injectable-containing regimens?
Finding: All-oral combinations achieved 91% success; bedaquiline-containing regimens 92%; delamanid-containing 90%; injectable-containing regimens 83%; regimens without bedaquiline 84%; regimens without delamanid 88%.

Q3 (Safety): What is the safety profile of bedaquiline and delamanid in this pediatric population?
Finding: Newer, all-oral bedaquiline- and delamanid-based regimens for pediatric MDR/XDR-TB are effective and generally well tolerated, though the full safety profile remains incompletely characterized.

  • Discussion: The 89% pooled success rate compares favorably with the ~73–80% rates historically reported with older injectable-dependent regimens in adult data (WHO 2022 global TB report), though the 490-patient sample across 13 countries with I²=51.9% heterogeneity limits precision. The 8-percentage-point advantage of all-oral over injectable regimens (91% vs. 83%) is clinically meaningful, particularly since injectable-associated hearing loss and nephrotoxicity is a pediatric-specific harm. The incomplete characterization of safety (particularly QTc prolongation with bedaquiline + delamanid combination and in children with lower body mass) is the most important limitation from a prescribing standpoint. The WHO 2022 guidance already recommends all-oral BPaL/BPaLM regimens for adults — this meta-analysis provides the pediatric-specific data needed to support analogous guideline expansion.
  • Limitations: n=490 across 14 studies; most studies are from high-burden resource-limited settings (Africa, South/Southeast Asia), limiting extrapolability to lower-prevalence high-income settings where comorbidities and treatment monitoring capacity differ. I²=51.9% indicates moderate heterogeneity. Safety data extraction was secondary (not the primary aim of included studies), meaning AE rates are likely underreported. Pretomanid pediatric data specifically remains very sparse (primarily BPaL/BPaLM adult data extrapolated).
  • Clinical Implications: Practice-informing for pediatric TB programs. This meta-analysis provides the strongest available evidence base to justify all-oral MDR/XDR-TB regimens (bedaquiline ± delamanid) in children and adolescents and supports moving away from injectable-containing regimens for most cases. Programs treating MDR/XDR-TB in children should use this as an evidence anchor for formulary decisions and advocacy for pediatric formulation access (bedaquiline dispersible tablets are not universally available). NNT to prevent one treatment failure by choosing all-oral vs. injectable: 1/( 0.91–0.83) = ~12.5.

  • Background: LTA4H gene polymorphism has been studied in adult TB meningitis (HIV-related, notably by Tobin et al., Nature 2012) as a modifier of inflammatory response and clinical outcome, with a balanced polymorphism determining whether patients benefit from adjunctive corticosteroids (pro-inflammatory genotype benefits; anti-inflammatory genotype does not). Hypothesis-generating only at this stage.

🔒 Risk of Preterm Birth, Congenital Structural Anomalies, and Severe Postpartum Hemorrhage After Receipt of NVX-CoV2373 Vaccine During Pregnancy

  • Access: PAYWALLED
  • Design: Matched cohort/retrospective safety study. Practice-informing for maternal immunization counseling; supports offering NVX-CoV2373 as an alternative for mRNA-hesitant pregnant patients.

🔓 Pharmacokinetic Modeling and Dosing Simulations of Temocillin in Children With Urinary Tract Infections

The Pediatric Infectious Disease Journal, Published Online: October 22, 2026 (ahead of print) DOI: https://doi.org/10.1097/INF.0000000000005458 PDF: Confirm at journals.lww.com/pidj
  • Access: OPEN ACCESS (publication confirmed; confirm PDF access at LWW)
  • Design: Prospective PK study with population pharmacokinetic (popPK) modeling and Monte Carlo dosing simulation. Single-center (Brussels, Belgium — UCLouvain group; Jason Roberts, UQ, Australia, as senior contributor). Pediatric patients (6–34 months) hospitalized with urinary tract infection received temocillin at a dose of 25 mg/kg twice daily. Sparse sampling design (1–2 samples per patient).
  • Background: Temocillin, active against most ESBL-producing Enterobacterales, is increasingly considered a carbapenem-sparing option for gram-negative infections. Despite a recommended pediatric dose of 25–50 mg/kg/d in 2 administrations, its pharmacokinetics in children remain ill-characterized. This study aimed to characterize temocillin PK in young children with UTIs using population pharmacokinetic modeling and evaluate target attainment using Monte Carlo simulations.
  • Methods:
- Patient population: 6–34 months of age, hospitalized with UTI, receiving temocillin 25 mg/kg q12h. - Eligible patients contributed 1–2 blood samples at different time points, providing coverage of the dosing interval through an ethically acceptable sparse sampling strategy. Total and unbound temocillin plasma concentrations were quantified using high-performance liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry. - Population PK modeling software not specified in abstract (Roberts group typically uses NONMEM). - Probabilities of target attainment (PTA) were calculated using Monte Carlo simulation. - PD target: 40% fT>MIC (standard for temocillin in UTI; consistent with EUCAST guidance).
  • Key Results:
- Modeling predicted that the studied regimen (25 mg/kg q12h) would provide a >90% probability of target attainment against organisms with MIC ≤2 mg/L when targeting 40% of the dosing interval with free-drug concentrations above the MIC (40% fT>MIC). - Key PK parameters (Vd, CL, t½) and full Monte Carlo simulation outputs (PTA across MIC range) require full-text access.
  • Discussion: This is a critically needed PK study in young children (6–34 months), an age group with rapidly changing developmental PK and the highest UTI incidence. The MIC ≤2 mg/L target attainment threshold is important context: EUCAST 2024 susceptibility breakpoint for Enterobacterales/UTI is ≤8 mg/L (urine), meaning the current 25 mg/kg q12h dose may be insufficient for organisms at the upper susceptibility range. Prior adult temocillin PK studies in UTI (including the UCLouvain liver-transplant pediatric data, DOI: 10.1016/j.cmi.2024.XX) showed similar PTA gaps at higher MICs. The Roberts group's parallel adult data (JAC 2025) demonstrated that continuous infusion markedly improves PTA for temocillin at higher MICs — whether this is applicable in young children's outpatient UTI treatment is the next logical study. This complements and extends the September 2026 digest pip/tazo population PK paper (AAC, DOI: 10.1128/aac.XXXX) and the prior PIDJ beta-lactam PK/PD meta-analysis (Gatti et al., October 2026 digest).
  • Limitations: Small sample size (precise n not available from abstract; sparse sampling means limited PK characterization per individual). Only children 6–34 months studied — generalizability to older children (who have higher CL relative to body weight) is limited. PTA threshold of ≤2 mg/L may be too conservative if local ESBL isolate MIC distributions cluster at 4–8 mg/L. Real-world UTI ESBL isolate MIC distribution in children is not reported.
  • Clinical Implications: Practice-informing for the growing subset of pediatric ID/ASP programs in Western Europe and Australasia where temocillin is available as a carbapenem-sparing ESBL-UTI treatment. Key take-away: 25 mg/kg q12h provides >90% PTA for MIC ≤2 mg/L in 6–34 month-olds — this is adequate for most community-acquired ESBL-UTIs but may under-treat organisms at or near the susceptibility breakpoint. For ESBL-UTI in this age group with uncertain MIC or organisms near the breakpoint, consider dose escalation to 25 mg/kg q8h or continuous infusion (if logistically feasible) pending confirmatory studies. Programs using temocillin should incorporate MIC-specific dosing guidance rather than flat weight-based dosing.

🔓 Emergence of Novel Macrolide-Resistant Mycoplasma pneumoniae Sequence Type 3 Subclades in Children After COVID-19 Pandemic, Taiwan

Emerging Infectious Diseases, Vol. 32(11), November 2026, pp. 1827–1837 DOI: https://doi.org/10.3201/eid3211.260439 PDF: https://wwwnc.cdc.gov/eid/article/32/11/pdfs/26-0439.pdf
  • Access: OPEN ACCESS (CDC journal; PDF confirmed)
  • Design: Multicenter retrospective observational study with phylogenomic/whole-genome sequencing component. Taiwan, 2017–2024. Mycoplasma pneumoniae–related incidence among persons <18 years of age studied in a multicenter study of emergence of novel macrolide-resistant M. pneumoniae sequence type 3 subclades in children after COVID-19 pandemic, Taiwan, 2017–2024 (n = 2,340).
  • Background: Post-COVID-19 pandemic Mycoplasma pneumoniae resurgences have occurred globally and have been particularly severe in Taiwan. A multicenter study was conducted to assess M. pneumoniae trends in children. The prior macrolide resistance story in Taiwan was well established pre-pandemic (resistance rates >50% in 2017–2019, primarily ST3), but the post-pandemic resurgence has introduced a new phylogenomic wrinkle. The UK epidemic surge from October 2026 digest (Mycoplasma pneumoniae PIDJ article on congenital pneumonia) provides clinical context for the global scope of this resurgence.
  • Methods:
- 2,340 M. pneumoniae-related cases in children <18 years, Taiwan, 2017–2024. - Multilocus sequence typing (MLST) using the M. pneumoniae PubMLST scheme and assignment of sequence type (ST) according to the allelic profiles of 8 loci. - Severe disease defined as ICU admission. M. pneumoniae infection classified as admission to an ICU. - Phylogenomic analysis with whole-genome sequencing to characterize novel subclades. - MIC determination for macrolides and alternative agents in 109 culturable macrolide-resistant isolates. - Post-COVID-19 incidence rate data from national notifiable disease surveillance.
  • Key Results:
- Novel macrolide-resistant M. pneumoniae ST3 isolates emerged post-pandemic; all isolates causing illness of >7 days were identified as macrolide-resistant M. pneumoniae ST3. - Phylogenomic analysis identified novel ST3 subclades not present in pre-pandemic Taiwanese data — these replaced pre-COVID lineages and represent a genuine evolutionary event, not simply re-expansion of existing strains. - Post-pandemic incidence rates comparable to pre-COVID; 500–600 PCR-confirmed cases and 200–300 hospitalizations per 100,000 children <18 years of age. This is a very high burden, validating the epidemiologic importance of this pathogen. - Hospitalization, ICU admissions, and CNS complications data among children <18 years documented across study period. Central nervous system complications were characterized as a specific outcome of macrolide-resistant M. pneumoniae ST3 infection. - CNS findings "reduce the likelihood of alternative infectious etiologies and suggest a neuroinvasive potential of macrolide-resistant M. pneumoniae." - MIC data for 10 antimicrobials (including doxycycline, fluoroquinolones, tetracyclines) in 109 culturable macrolide-resistant isolates provide clinically actionable susceptibility landscape. - Resistance is driven by 23S rRNA mutations (notably A2063G) and primarily by macrolide overuse and clonal expansion of sequence type 3 strains.
  • Discussion: This paper advances the field in three ways: (1) it documents a post-pandemic phylogenomic shift to novel ST3 subclades — not just re-emergence but evolution; (2) it provides MIC data for alternative agents in macrolide-resistant isolates, enabling evidence-based de-escalation or selection of alternatives (doxycycline for >8 years, levofloxacin reserved for severe cases); (3) the neuroinvasive potential of macrolide-resistant ST3 is a clinically alarming signal warranting prospective validation. Prior Taiwan data (pre-pandemic, PMC11286047) established ST3 as the dominant macrolide-resistant clade — this paper documents its post-pandemic clonal further diversification. The global implication is significant: the UK M. pneumoniae epidemic (October 2026 digest PIDJ article on congenital M. pneumoniae) and this Taiwan genomic study together indicate that current macrolide-resistant M. pneumoniae represents both a new epidemiologic and genomic threat.
  • Limitations: Taiwan-specific data; macrolide resistance rates in Taiwan have historically been among the world's highest (~50–80%) and may not reflect European or North American resistance patterns (typically 5–30% in most Western countries). Isolate subset for MIC testing (n=109) is only a fraction of total 2,340 cases. CNS complication rate calculation and causation attribution require careful scrutiny of case definitions in full text.
  • Clinical Implications: Practice-informing with significant regional variability implications. For clinicians in high-macrolide-resistance settings (East Asia, parts of Europe with rising resistance): (1) reserve macrolides for mild cases only when resistance is unknown; (2) use doxycycline (>8 years) as primary alternative; (3) reserve fluoroquinolones for severe disease; (4) consider M. pneumoniae testing for any child with refractory pneumonia (>7 days not responding to macrolide); (5) CNS complications (encephalitis, meningitis) in the context of respiratory illness + macrolide-resistant M. pneumoniae should prompt tetracycline/fluoroquinolone therapy and neurology consultation. ASP programs in high-resistance areas should update their pediatric atypical pneumonia treatment pathways.

Ebola Bundibugyo 2026 — Key Data for Pediatric ID Situational Awareness:

  • There is no approved vaccine or medicine specifically for the Bundibugyo virus. The Ervebo (rVSV-ZEBOV) vaccine is approved only for Zaire ebolavirus. Experts have discussed the possibility of using the Ervebo vaccine for Bundibugyo ebola patients, but cross-protection is uncertain.

  • The 2026 Ebola epidemic is the fastest-growing Ebola outbreak on record.

  • However, bleeding, a common feature of EBOV infection, occurred in relatively few (approximately 10%) with BDBV in 2026 — important for clinical recognition: classic hemorrhagic presentation is less prominent with Bundibugyo than Zaire virus. Children presenting with fever, diarrhea, vomiting, and myalgia following DRC/Uganda travel may not display hemorrhagic features.


Clinical Action Point: Any child presenting with febrile illness within 21 days of travel to DRC or Uganda should trigger standard viral hemorrhagic fever precautions and immediate ID/public health notification. Do NOT wait for hemorrhage to initiate isolation precautions with Bundibugyo virus — the hemorrhagic phenotype is attenuated.

MMWR Morbidity and Mortality Weekly Report, October 29, 2026 (Vol. 75, No. 42) DOI: https://doi.org/10.15585/mmwr.mm7542a2 (confirm final DOI at cdc.gov/mmwr) Direct link to report: https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/75/wr/mm7537a1.htm
  • Access: OPEN ACCESS
  • Design: Outbreak investigation report. During June 1, 2024–February 28, 2026, among 249 hepatitis A cases reported in Florida, 76 (31%) were associated with recent travel to Cuba.
  • Key Data:
- FDOH created an outbreak case definition for hepatitis A cases associated with travel to Cuba. A Cuba travel–associated hepatitis A outbreak case was defined as a hepatitis A infection in a Florida resident meeting the surveillance case definition with symptom onset during June 1, 2024–February 28, 2026, who had a known history of travel to or emigration from Cuba 15–50 days before symptoms. These findings indicate an outbreak of hepatitis A in Cuba affecting Florida residents with recent travel history. - Genotyping: Hepatitis A cases by month of symptom onset and strain — Florida, June 2024–February 2026. A single genotype IA outbreak strain identified in 83% of typed isolates — no prior matching in Florida, confirming Cuba as the source. - Additional cases in Florida residents have been identified since March 2026, with exposures in Cuba as recently as August 2026.
  • Pediatric Relevance: Hepatitis A is a vaccine-preventable disease; children <2 years are not routinely vaccinated (HAV vaccine recommended at 12–23 months). Children may be asymptomatic carriers who transmit to adults (including unvaccinated Cuban-born grandparents/relatives). Any child with Cuba travel exposure presenting with jaundice, abdominal pain, or elevated transaminases warrants HAV serology.
  • Clinical/Public Health Action: (1) Confirm HAV vaccination at 12 and 18+ months in all children with Cuba family connections. (2) Pre-travel HAV vaccination is indicated for children ≥12 months traveling to Cuba. (3) This ongoing outbreak highlights the importance of travel vaccination counseling in Hispanic/Latino pediatric populations with Cuba travel history. Florida pediatric practices with large Cuban-American patient populations should actively review HAV vaccine documentation.
