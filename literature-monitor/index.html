Empirical Antibiotic Activity and Outcomes in Pediatric Gram-Negative Bloodstream Infections: A Rank-Based Composite Outcome Analysis
Clinical Infectious Diseases, Vol. 83, Issue 2, August 15, 2026, pp. e282–e290 | https://doi.org/10.1093/cid/ciag234
PDF: Paywalled

  • Access: PAYWALLED
  • Design: Multicenter prospective cohort study; PAEDS Network, Australia; n= not fully disclosed in abstract but described as a multicenter pediatric GNBSI cohort; dates of data collection span multiple sites in a high-income country setting.
  • Background: In adults, active empirical therapy (AET) — defined as at least one in-vitro active antibiotic within 48–72 hours of blood culture draw — has long been considered a pillar of BSI management. However, in high-income settings, low pediatric mortality following gram-negative bloodstream infection (GNBSI) limits its value as a sole endpoint. Prior observational data have generally been derived from adult cohorts, where GNBSI mortality is 15–30%; pediatric studies have been hampered by insufficient sample size to detect mortality differences, driving interest in composite outcome approaches.
  • Methods: The authors used a rank-based composite outcome ("win ratio" methodology), incorporating multiple clinically relevant outcomes hierarchically ordered by clinical priority (e.g., death > ICU admission > length of stay > antimicrobial escalation). AET was defined as receipt of at least one in-vitro active antibiotic within the first 3 days of GNBSI onset; comparator was inactive empirical therapy (IET). Propensity weighting (inverse probability of treatment weighting) was used to adjust for confounders, and stratified analyses were conducted by comorbidity burden and acquisition category (community- vs. healthcare-associated).
  • Key Findings: Overall, AET within the first 3 days of GNBSI onset was not associated with improved outcomes compared with IET, and stratified analyses by comorbidity and acquisition category similarly showed no significant differences. In this multicenter pediatric GNBSI cohort, active empirical therapy was not associated with improved outcomes. The impact of AET in high-resource settings may be smaller than assumed, though timely effective therapy remains critical for severe illness and low-resource settings.
  • Discussion: This is one of the first rigorous pediatric-specific datasets to directly challenge the assumption that AET universally improves GNBSI outcomes across all pediatric patients. These findings are consistent with emerging adult data suggesting the mortality benefit of AET is most pronounced in patients with septic shock and high severity of illness, and may be attenuated in less ill patients managed in high-resource settings. The win-ratio composite methodology is a methodological strength — it captures a broader range of clinically meaningful outcomes than mortality alone.
  • Limitations: The primary threat to validity is unmeasured confounding: children who received IET may have had less severe illness or more reliable source control (e.g., catheter removal). Generalizability to low- and middle-income country settings is explicitly noted as limited. The definition of "active" therapy based on in-vitro susceptibility may not fully capture pharmacodynamic adequacy. Restriction to high-income Australian centers may underestimate the benefit of AET seen globally.
  • Clinical Implications: Practice-informing, not practice-changing. This study does NOT justify withholding appropriate empirical coverage in children with suspected GNBSI and clinical instability or immunocompromise. Rather, it provides data to support narrowed empirical regimens and prompt de-escalation once susceptibilities return in clinically stable pediatric patients in resource-adequate settings. ASP programs should use this to push back against reflexive broad-spectrum escalation for GNBSI in well-appearing children without septic physiology.

Adenovirus vs. RSV Severity in the PICU: A Multicenter Latin American Cohort Study
The Pediatric Infectious Disease Journal, Published online August 26, 2026 | https://doi.org/[PIDJ online ahead of print — DOI to be confirmed upon issue assignment; journal listing confirms August 26, 2026 publication date]
PDF: Paywalled — see https://www.ovid.com/jnls/pidj

  • Access: PAYWALLED
  • Design: Multicenter retrospective/prospective cohort; 37 PICUs in 7 Latin American countries (2017–2025); n=3,196 PICU admissions; children aged 1 month to 18 years. This is likely the LARed Network.
  • Background: RSV is the canonical driver of pediatric PICU respiratory admissions globally. Adenovirus is detected less often, but severe pneumonia may occur. The pediatric intensive care unit (PICU) course associated with adenovirus detection compared with RSV-only detection is not well defined. Prior single-center and small cohort data suggested adenovirus carries higher PICU mortality than RSV, but large multicenter comparative data across resource-variable settings were lacking.
  • Methods: Children were studied from 37 PICUs in 7 Latin American countries (2017–2025). Children with RSV and/or adenovirus detection who used respiratory support were grouped as RSV-only, adenovirus-only, or RSV-adenovirus codetection. The primary outcome was PICU death or worse functional status at discharge. Adjusted associations were estimated with logistic regression; Firth penalization was used for mortality. Firth penalization is appropriate here given the relatively small number of adenovirus deaths, preventing complete separation in logistic models — this is a methodological strength.
  • Key Findings: Among 3,196 PICU admissions, 2,859 had RSV-only, 257 adenovirus-only, and 80 codetection. PICU mortality was 12/257 (4.7%) in adenovirus-only, 13/2,859 (0.5%) in RSV-only, and 2/80 (2.5%) in codetection. Compared with RSV-only detection, adenovirus-only detection was associated with mortality (adjusted OR: 10.56; 95% CI: 4.57–24.43) and with PICU death or worse functional status at discharge (21/222 [9.5%] vs. 45/2,308 [1.9%]; adjusted OR: 6.57; 95% CI: 3.59–12.03). Among nonsurvivors, the median PICU day of death was 3 (IQR: 2–6) with adenovirus-only detection and 8 (IQR: 3–9) with RSV-only detection.
  • Discussion: The near tenfold adjusted odds of mortality with adenovirus vs. RSV in PICU patients requiring respiratory support is striking and consistent with prior smaller series. The earlier median day of death (day 3 vs. day 8) in adenovirus patients suggests more fulminant disease course, potentially implicating direct viral cytopathology rather than secondary bacterial superinfection as the primary driver of early mortality. This has therapeutic implications: there is currently no approved antiviral for severe adenovirus disease, though cidofovir and brincidofovir have been used off-label in immunocompromised patients. Whether these agents confer benefit in immunocompetent children with severe adenoviral PICU disease remains an open clinical question. The RSV-adenovirus codetection group (n=80) showed intermediate mortality (2.5%), insufficient to draw firm conclusions, though the point estimate suggests codetection did not synergistically worsen outcomes beyond adenovirus alone.
  • Limitations: The primary limitation is that "detection" in clinician-ordered specimens (rather than systematic surveillance testing) introduces significant selection bias — children tested for adenovirus were likely perceived as having atypical or severe presentations, inflating the apparent severity differential. The 8-year time span (2017–2025) encompasses substantial changes in PICU supportive care, viral epidemiology (COVID-19 disruption of normal seasonality), and testing practices. Resource heterogeneity across 7 countries with widely varying PICU infrastructure introduces important confounding. Causality cannot be established.
  • Clinical Implications: Practice-informing. Adenoviral PICU admission should prompt early consideration of antiviral options in immunocompromised hosts, urgent immune status assessment, and proactive escalation planning (ECMO consultation in deteriorating patients). Antibiotic stewardship perspective: the very high PICU mortality associated with adenovirus supports not inappropriately reassuring clinicians that "it's just a virus" — early deterioration warrants aggressive supportive care, not antibiotic escalation. This extends work on viral coinfection severity from the prior digest's PIDJ June 2026 retrospective cohort study, where virus-pair specificity (not additive coinfection) drove severity outcomes; here, single-pathogen adenovirus in the PICU is dramatically more dangerous than RSV alone.

Cytomegalovirus Shedding Among CMV-Seropositive Women Living with HIV on cART and Its Impact on Infant CMV Infection
The Pediatric Infectious Disease Journal, Published online August 26, 2026 | https://www.ovid.com/jnls/pidj
PDF: Paywalled

  • Access: PAYWALLED
  • Design: Prospective cohort; n=150 CMV-seropositive pregnant women living with HIV (WLHIV) on combination antiretroviral therapy (cART) and 122 non-breastfed infants; single or multicenter; non-breastfeeding setting (implies resource-adequate country).
  • Background: CMV shedding among CMV-seropositive women living with HIV on combination antiretroviral therapy (cART) and its impact on infant CMV infection in non-breastfeeding settings remains unclear. Congenital CMV (cCMV) is the leading infectious cause of sensorineural hearing loss and neurodevelopmental disability. Among WLHIV, the intersection of maternal HIV infection and immune reconstitution on cART with CMV shedding dynamics is incompletely characterized. Most prior data come from breastfeeding-permitted settings where postnatal transmission is a major confounder.
  • Methods: Maternal CMV urinary shedding was assessed from pregnancy through 6 weeks postpartum and timing and incidence of infant CMV transmission were determined over 12 months. Data from 150 CMV-seropositive pregnant WLHIV receiving cART and 122 non-breastfed infants enrolled in a cohort study were analyzed. CMV-DNA was measured in maternal and infant urine using real-time polymerase chain reaction. Incidence of maternal and infant viral shedding and its correlates were analyzed. Twenty-nine of the 150 women (19.3%; 95% CI: 13.0%–25.6%) shed CMV-DNA (median 2.44 log10 copies/mL).
  • Key Findings: Maternal CMV urinary shedding prevalence was 19.3% among WLHIV on suppressive cART. The study characterizes timing of infant CMV acquisition over 12 months in a non-breastfeeding setting, isolating the contribution of in-utero vs. intrapartum routes. Specific infant transmission rates and temporal patterns are available only in the full text.
  • Discussion: The 19.3% maternal urinary shedding rate among virologically suppressed WLHIV is notable — cART does not fully suppress CMV reactivation even in the setting of controlled HIV. This has implications for cCMV screening and prevention strategies in this population. The non-breastfeeding design provides cleaner estimates of vertical (in-utero/intrapartum) CMV transmission risk, unconfounded by postnatal breast milk exposure.
  • Limitations: Urine-only CMV testing (vs. saliva or blood) may underestimate shedding. Selection bias if enrollment favored virologically suppressed women. Single-country generalizability unknown.
  • Clinical Implications: Practice-informing. WLHIV on cART still shed CMV in ~20% of pregnancies. Routine cCMV screening in infants born to WLHIV should be considered, particularly given the high rate of CMV reactivation even on suppressive therapy. This extends prior PIDJ August 2026 RSV perinatal cohort work, now addressing a distinct vertical transmission pathway (CMV) in the same PIDJ volume.

[MMWR Report 1] RSV Immunization Effectiveness and Safety for the 2026–2027 Respiratory Season — Vaccine Integrity Project Evidence Review

JAMA, Published September 2, 2026 | https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2026.17871
PDF: Open access token available via JAMA media release; https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/fullarticle/10.1001/jama.2026.17871

  • Access: OPEN ACCESS (with media token)
  • Design: Systematic review summarizing evidence from recent randomized clinical trials and observational studies reporting on the effectiveness and safety of respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) vaccines and monoclonal antibodies, as well as the epidemiology of RSV in the US. Part of the three-review Vaccine Integrity Project package published simultaneously in JAMA (also including influenza and COVID-19 reviews). The review, conducted in collaboration with the American Medical Association, evaluated newly published evidence on the epidemiology, effectiveness, and safety of US-licensed influenza, COVID-19, and RSV vaccines and immunizations ahead of the 2026–27 respiratory virus season. Together, the three reviews identified 299 eligible studies published primarily between August 2025 and June 2026.
  • Background: RSV prevention in infants has been transformed by nirsevimab (long-acting monoclonal antibody) and maternal RSVpreF vaccine (Abrysvo), both widely implemented in the 2024–2025 season. Real-world effectiveness data have rapidly accumulated, but an annual synthesis is needed to inform policy recommendations for the upcoming 2026–2027 season. This is the second annual Vaccine Integrity Project RSV review.
  • Methods: Systematic review of RCTs and observational studies; studies published primarily August 2025–June 2026; outcomes included effectiveness against RSV-related ED visits, hospitalization, and ICU admission for nirsevimab and maternal vaccine; safety outcomes included Guillain-Barré syndrome (GBS), preterm birth, and other adverse events of special interest.
  • Key Findings:
- RSV immunizations were associated with reduced risk of severe RSV and RSV-related hospitalizations; evidence among older adults suggests that RSV vaccine effectiveness may be lower in subsequent seasons. - Reported adverse events of special interest (including GBS and preterm birth) were consistent with prior reviews — no new safety signals identified. - Real-world effectiveness data from prior seasons: the JAMA Pediatrics 2025 dataset (7 U.S. sites, 2024–2025) showed nirsevimab effectiveness of 81% and maternal vaccine effectiveness of 70% against RSV hospitalizations. RSV hospitalization rates in 2024–2025 were reduced by an estimated 41%–51% among newborns and infants aged 0–11 months compared to 2017–2020 rates or estimated expected rates in 2024–2025; the highest reduction of 56%–63% occurred in newborns and infants aged 0–2 months. - The living meta-analysis (Thorax, 2025) covering ~7.6 million people showed nirsevimab at 80.7% effectiveness (95% CI: 75.7%–85.7%; 7 studies) against RSV-related ED visits, 80.7% (95% CI: 76.1%–85.2%; 17 studies) against hospital admissions, and 75.6% (95% CI: 63.3%–87.9%; 8 studies) against ICU admissions. - During the 2025–2026 season, CDC estimates indicated that influenza was associated with 390,000–800,000 hospitalizations. COVID-19 hospitalization rates continued their decline from previous seasons, but the highest hospitalization rates remained concentrated among older adults and infants.
  • PICO Questions & Recommendations:
Q1: In infants in their first RSV season, does nirsevimab reduce RSV-associated hospitalizations vs. no prophylaxis? Recommendation: Yes — nirsevimab recommended for all infants in their first RSV season (AAP/ACIP maintained recommendation for 2026–2027). Strength / Quality: Strong; multiple real-world studies (pooled 80.7% VE against hospitalization, 17 studies). Supporting data: Pooled VE 80.7% (95% CI: 76.1%–85.2%) against hospitalization; 17 observational studies; no new safety concerns.

Q2: In pregnant women 32–36 weeks gestation, does maternal RSVpreF vaccine (Abrysvo) reduce infant RSV hospitalizations vs. placebo?
Recommendation: Maternal RSV vaccine remains recommended for pregnant women at 32–36 weeks gestation during RSV season.
Strength / Quality: Strong; RCT-demonstrated; real-world VE 70% against hospitalization (7-site U.S. cohort 2024–2025).
Supporting data: Maternal VE 70% (95% CI not provided in this digest) against RSV hospitalization; prior Australian FluCAN-PAEDS data showed 80.8% VE (reviewed in June 2026 digest).

Q3: Is there a safety signal (GBS, preterm birth) with currently recommended RSV immunizations?
Recommendation: No new safety signals. Continue current recommendations.
Strength / Quality: Consistent with established safety profile across multiple comparative studies.
Supporting data: Adverse events of special interest consistent with prior reviews; no new signals.

Q4: Does nirsevimab effectiveness wane across the RSV season?
Recommendation (observation, not directive): Evidence among older adults suggests waning VE in subsequent seasons; pediatric waning data currently insufficient to change timing recommendations.
Supporting data: Prior living meta-analysis: waning effectiveness with observation time ≥150 days (breakthrough risk RR 2.17, 95% CI 1.86–2.53 in seasons >150 days); this pattern may be less clinically relevant in infants who receive nirsevimab annually.

  • Discussion: This annual synthesis effectively synthesizes the now-robust real-world evidence base supporting RSV immunization in infants. The Australian nirsevimab effectiveness data reviewed in the June 2026 digest (82–90% VE) are consistent with the pooled estimates here, providing important international validation. The stewardship angle — previously highlighted in the August 6, 2026 CID nirsevimab-antibiotic paper (now reviewed in the August 9–23 digest) showing 14.4% reduction in outpatient antibiotic prescribing — is not addressed in this evidence review but remains a compelling secondary benefit argument.
  • Limitations: Systematic reviews of observational effectiveness studies are susceptible to healthy vaccinee bias (parents who obtain nirsevimab may have better healthcare access). Heterogeneity across RSV seasons (2023–2024 vs. 2024–2025) introduces variability. Waning effectiveness data in infants remain limited to single-season follow-up.
  • Clinical Implications: Practice-confirming. Maintain current recommendations: nirsevimab for all infants in first RSV season; maternal RSVpreF vaccine for pregnant women at 32–36 weeks during RSV season. Prepare formulary and logistics for the 2026–2027 season NOW. For ASP programs: the secondary antibiotic-reduction benefit of nirsevimab (per August 6 CID study) provides a stewardship argument for institutional support of immunization access programs.

[MMWR Report 2] Vaccination Coverage Among Adolescents Aged 13–17 Years — National Immunization Survey-Teen (NIS-Teen), United States, 2025

MMWR Morbidity and Mortality Weekly Report, Vol. 75, No. 34, September 3, 2026 | https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/75/wr/mm7534a1.htm
PDF: https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/75/wr/pdfs/mm7534-H.pdf

  • Access: OPEN ACCESS
  • Design: Cross-sectional, population-based national survey; 18,692 adolescents in the 2025 NIS-Teen, born January 2007–December 2012 (i.e., aged 13–17 years in 2025); two-phase design with random-digit-dialed mobile telephone household survey followed by provider record confirmation; adolescents from Guam, Puerto Rico, and the U.S. Virgin Islands excluded from national estimates. National and state/local jurisdiction-level coverage estimates produced.
  • Background: Annual NIS-Teen data provide the primary monitoring mechanism for adolescent vaccination coverage in the U.S. Against the backdrop of an ongoing measles emergency (3,134 confirmed measles cases as of September 3, 2026, spanning 47 jurisdictions), understanding the underlying vaccination coverage gaps driving outbreak vulnerability is essential. The 2024 NIS-Teen (published August 2025) showed MMR coverage at 92.5% — below the 95% Healthy People 2030 threshold — and persistent HPV stagnation.
  • Methods: Random-digit-dialed mobile survey with provider-verified vaccination records; national and jurisdiction-level estimates with 95% confidence intervals; coverage compared with Healthy People 2030 targets; stratified by MSA status (urban, suburban, rural), poverty level, and age.
  • Key Findings:
- Coverage with both HPV and measles, mumps, and rubella (MMR) vaccines remained below Healthy People 2030 targets. - HPV: In 2025, 58.6% of adolescents aged 13–15 years had completed the HPV vaccination series, which is below the Healthy People 2030 target of 80% of adolescents aged 13–15 years receiving the recommended number of HPV vaccine doses. - MMR: Coverage with ≥2 doses of MMR vaccine among adolescents aged 13–17 years was 92.5% in 2025, below the Healthy People 2030 target of 95% coverage at kindergarten entry, suggesting that catch-up vaccination among adolescents did not meet the Healthy People 2030 target, the estimated population-level immunity necessary to prevent measles outbreaks. - MenACWY: Coverage with ≥1 MenACWY dose was 3.0 percentage points lower among adolescents living in non-metropolitan (rural) areas vs. MSA principal cities. - Vaccination coverage varied by jurisdiction, with the largest variation observed for HPV vaccine. - Survey sample: n=18,692 adolescents; comparable methodology to prior NIS-Teen cycles, enabling year-over-year comparisons.
  • Discussion: The persistent failure to achieve 95% MMR coverage among U.S. adolescents — at the exact moment the country is experiencing 3,134 measles cases in 2026 alone — is the defining public health context for interpreting these data. There have been 38 new outbreaks reported in 2026, and 95% of confirmed cases (2,969 of 3,134) are outbreak-associated. The 92.5% adolescent MMR coverage means approximately 1 in 14 adolescents remains susceptible, clustering in specific geographic regions and social networks to create outbreak-permissive conditions. HPV coverage stagnation at 58.6% series completion represents a multi-year trend now directly translating into preventable JoRRP cases (see PIDJ article above) and future HPV-associated cancer burden. Rural adolescents have consistently lower MenACWY coverage — a critical vulnerability given the FDA's April 2026 approval of MenQuadfi for infants as young as 6 weeks (reviewed in the April 2026 digest).
Compared to the 2024 NIS-Teen (August 2025 MMWR): MMR coverage held at 92.5% (flat, no improvement year-over-year). HPV series completion is essentially unchanged from the 2023–2024 period of stagnation for the third consecutive year.
  • Limitations: Provider-record verification is the gold standard but requires parental consent, introducing potential non-response bias toward more engaged, health-literate families (which would overestimate true coverage). Coverage estimates reflect reported vaccination; immunogenicity gaps (e.g., cold-chain failures) are not captured. Annual point estimates do not capture within-year declines in coverage following policy changes.
  • Clinical Implications: Directly practice-relevant and action-required at the clinic level.
- Every pediatric ID encounter is an opportunity to identify MMR non-compliance and administer catch-up vaccination. - HPV coverage: pediatric ID physicians should provide a strong recommendation at every encounter with eligible adolescents, given well-documented data showing physician recommendation as the primary driver of HPV vaccination uptake. - The rural MenACWY gap warrants targeted outreach through school-based clinics and rural health systems, particularly given the FDA's expansion of MenACWY indications. - ASP connection: improving HPV coverage directly reduces JoRRP incidence and the associated repeated surgical procedures, anesthetic exposures, and adjuvant antiviral treatments (cidofovir, etc.) these children require.

[MMWR Report 3] Use of Nowcasting to Estimate Real-Time Transmission Trends During a Measles Outbreak — South Carolina, October 2025–March 2026

MMWR Morbidity and Mortality Weekly Report, Vol. 75, No. 33, August 27, 2026 | https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/75/wr/mm7533a1.htm
PDF: https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/75/wr/pdfs/mm7533a1-H.pdf

  • Access: OPEN ACCESS
  • Design: Outbreak investigation with retrospective epidemiological modeling analysis; CDC developed and validated a Bayesian nowcasting model applied to the largest measles outbreak in the United States in 3 decades: 997 cases were reported. Time period: October 2025–March 2026, South Carolina. The South Carolina Department of Public Health (SCDPH) provided daily case line lists; CDC applied the nowcasting model in near-real-time and retrospectively validated against finalized case data.
  • Background: Measles is a highly contagious viral disease. Large measles outbreaks are becoming increasingly common in the United States, often affecting communities with low vaccination coverage. Reporting delays can complicate real-time assessments of outbreak trends. Nowcasting uses models to adjust for incomplete reported data based on historical reporting delays, to estimate current trends in disease metrics. CDC has applied nowcasting to routine respiratory surveillance data, but rarely in an outbreak setting. The South Carolina outbreak provided the first large-scale test of outbreak nowcasting for a vaccine-preventable disease in the U.S.
  • Methods: CDC developed a nowcast model that corrected for reporting delays to estimate real-time case counts and the effective reproduction number (Rt). The model used historical reporting delay distributions from the SCDPH line list to generate posterior probability estimates of daily case counts and Rt categories (increasing: Rt probability ≥95%; likely increasing: 60–95%; stable/decreasing: <60%). Calibration assessed using coverage of 90% prediction intervals (well-calibrated model should cover true value ~90% of time). Model was applied weekly using successive line list receipts.
  • Key Findings:
- Total outbreak size: 997 confirmed cases, October 2025–March 2026. - Despite missing this shift [in January], nowcasts improved estimates of total outbreak size by 10.5%–11.3% compared with provisional counts. - Transmission increased rapidly at the end of December, likely due to changes in contact patterns around holiday gatherings, which the model did not account for. - Key operational success: Nowcasts provided reassurance that the declines observed in late January were true declines rather than artifacts of delayed reporting, enabling de-escalation of SCDPH response efforts during February and March. - After January 23, 2026, nowcasts captured cumulative and daily case counts within 90% PIs, coinciding with high reporting (72.1%–88.5% of final cases). - Model limitation: During January, when reporting completeness dropped sharply, it underestimated cases and missed the outbreak peak. The model failed to detect the true January peak (cases peaked January 13–14) in the January 16 and 23 data.
  • Discussion: This is one of the most important methodological public health papers in recent memory for outbreak response. The core operational value: if essential date fields are consistently collected, nowcasting can support situational awareness and decisions about response capacity by providing real-time estimates of daily case counts and transmission trends. The model failure in January — when a rapid case surge coincided with reporting backlog — illustrates that nowcasting models are only as good as the stability of their underlying delay assumptions. The holiday-period contact rate change represented an unmodeled perturbation. Practically, the model failed precisely when accurate situational awareness was most needed (during the peak). However, the model's success in the de-escalation phase — distinguishing true decline from reporting artifact — provided real operational value and enabled appropriate response demobilization.
Current measles context: As of September 3, 2026, 3,134 confirmed measles cases were reported in the United States in 2026, representing the worst year for measles in decades. As of August 27, 2026, 2,903 confirmed measles cases were reported in the United States in 2026, among these, 2,887 measles cases were reported by 47 jurisdictions.
  • Limitations: Single-outbreak validation — the model's performance in different outbreak contexts (different geographic scales, different pathogens, different reporting systems) is unknown. The model required consistent date field collection, which is not guaranteed across all public health reporting jurisdictions. Nowcasting is retrospective validation of a prospective application; it cannot be validated until the outbreak ends. The model does not account for time-varying contact rates (holidays, school opening/closing) — a known limitation for seasonal outbreaks.
  • Clinical Implications: Primarily of public health/hospital epidemiology programmatic relevance.
- Pediatric ID physicians should understand that provisional case counts during outbreaks systematically underestimate true current case burden — "the dashboard is always behind." - Although accuracy depended on the stability of case reporting patterns, the model improved situational awareness and provided more reliable real-time signals than did provisional case counts alone. - Institutional implication: hospitals and health departments receiving daily measles case feeds should discount provisional totals by 10–11% during active outbreak periods and should not interpret apparent case declines as definitive without nowcasting adjustment. - Nowcasting framework is generalizable: consider applying to pertussis, RSV, or influenza outbreak monitoring in institutional settings.

[MMWR Report 4] Notes from the Field: Characteristics and Monitoring of the 2026 Outbreak of Ebola Disease Caused by Bundibugyo Virus — Democratic Republic of the Congo, August 2026

MMWR Morbidity and Mortality Weekly Report, Published September 1, 2026 (online) | https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/75/wr/mm7535e1.htm
PDF: Open access (MMWR)

  • Access: OPEN ACCESS
  • Background: On May 14, 2026, an epidemic of Ebola was reported in the Ituri Province of the Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC). It is the 17th Ebola outbreak in the DRC and began five months after the end of the previous outbreak. Early infections are theorized to have occurred in January or February 2026 in the town of Mongbwalu. It is caused by the Bundibugyo ebolavirus (BDBV), which has complicated efforts as existing treatments and vaccines are for the Zaire ebolavirus. The outbreak was declared a public health emergency of international concern (PHEIC) by the World Health Organization (WHO) on May 16, 2026.
  • Current Outbreak Status (as of August 21, 2026):
- The 2026 Ebola DRC outbreak caused by Bundibugyo virus is now the second largest Ebola outbreak ever recorded. As of August 21, 2026, DRC reported 5,458 confirmed cases and 2,606 (48%) confirmed deaths. - Compared with previous Ebola outbreaks, the increase in cases in DRC is unprecedented, with approximately 5,000 cases in 100 days. - Cases have been reported from six of the 26 DRC provinces (Bas-Uélé, Haut-Uélé, Ituri, North Kivu, South Kivu, and Tshopo), affecting 57 of 151 health zones in the affected provinces. Ituri province remains the outbreak epicenter, accounting for 84% of reported cases. - Updated ECDC data (September 3, 2026): The DRC reported a total of 6,342 confirmed cases, including 3,072 related deaths. A total of 770 patients are hospitalized in isolation.
  • Response Gaps:
- The targets for five critical public health response indicators (case detection alerts, contact tracing, laboratory testing, isolation of infected persons, and safe and dignified burials) have not yet been met, and the outbreak continues to expand rapidly. - There have been 3,072 confirmed deaths in the DRC, and another 2 in Uganda as of the first week of September. On May 22, 2026, Africa CDC recommended molecular diagnostic testing (using real-time PCR) for identifying the Bundibugyo ebolavirus strain, because no antigen rapid diagnostic test (RDT) met WHO target product profile specifications for recommendation during the outbreak response. - On July 10, 2026, the US CDC reported that a US citizen working for a humanitarian organization in DRC had tested positive for Bundibugyo virus. The patient was medically evacuated to Germany on July 13, 2026.
  • Key Distinction from Prior Zaire ebolavirus Outbreaks: The absence of an approved vaccine or proven antiviral therapy for Bundibugyo virus (BDBV) is the defining therapeutic constraint. Zmapp, remdesivir, mAb114, and REGN-EB3 — the agents demonstrating benefit in the 2018–2020 Zaire ebolavirus outbreak — have no proven efficacy against BDBV. Management remains supportive, emphasizing fluid resuscitation, electrolyte management, and treatment of secondary infections.
  • U.S./Pediatric Clinical Implications:
- U.S. pediatric risk remains low. No pediatric cases imported to the U.S. have been confirmed. - All U.S. children traveling to DRC or Uganda should be assessed for Bundibugyo virus risk; travel medicine consultation recommended. - Prepare hospital EVD protocols for potential returning traveler or medical evacuee. Key pediatric management consideration: high case fatality rate (48%) emphasizes aggressive supportive care. - Laboratory diagnostic alert: no approved BDBV-specific RDT. PCR is required for diagnosis. Pediatric fever returning from endemic provinces warrants immediate biosafety level assessment and public health notification prior to specimen processing. - This MMWR report extends the June 2026 digest's coverage of Bundibugyo modeling scenarios; the outbreak has grown nearly 15-fold in confirmed cases since those initial projections (378 cases in June → 6,342 as of September 3, 2026).
