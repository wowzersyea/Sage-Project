This is an advance article and will update its citation information once assigned to a CID issue. A companion article specifically addressing the use of tPA and DNase (or tPA alone) for fibrinolysis in pediatric complicated CAP was simultaneously released (DOI: https://doi.org/10.1093/cid/ciag191).

This guideline was published prior to the digest window and is therefore not reviewed in full here. Programs that have not yet operationalized these recommendations should note:

  • As the first part of an update to the clinical practice guideline on the management of CAP in infants and children older than 3 months of age, the guideline presents six updated recommendations spanning the characterization and management of pneumonia with parapneumonic effusion.
  • The 2026 guideline replaces the previous 2011 guideline. The 2011 guideline featured more than 90 recommendations; the 2026 guideline is more focused, featuring five key sections and six recommendations.
  • The panel's recommendations are based on evidence derived from systematic literature reviews and adhere to the GRADE approach.
  • The scope of this guideline includes the diagnosis and management of pneumonia with parapneumonic effusion in children. Complicated pneumonia, including parapneumonic effusion, empyema, lung abscess, and necrotizing pneumonia, occurs in a small portion of children with pneumonia but is associated with higher rates of morbidity.
Access: DOI https://doi.org/10.1093/cid/ciag186 (Part 1) and https://doi.org/10.1093/cid/ciag191 (Part 2 — fibrinolysis). Both are available at Oxford Academic. Access status should be confirmed at the institutional level; the IDSA website provides free access to member institutions. Full guideline-format review is deferred to programs that have not yet implemented (prior to this digest's window). Clinical bottom line: Pediatric ID and hospital medicine programs that manage complicated CAP should confirm local protocols are concordant with the 2026 update, particularly regarding pleural drainage indications, fibrinolytic agent selection, and duration of antibiotic therapy for empyema.

[Article 1] Congenital Syphilis Diagnosis and Management at a Tertiary Care Center in Alabama: Concordance with CDC Recommendations

The Pediatric Infectious Disease Journal, Published Online September 14, 2026 | https://doi.org/10.1097/INF.0000000000005XXX (confirm final DOI at journals.lww.com/pidj)
PDF: Paywalled

  • Access: PAYWALLED

  • Design: Retrospective cohort study, single-center (University of Alabama at Birmingham, tertiary care), infants diagnosed with congenital syphilis (CS), 2013–2023 (10-year period).

  • Background: Congenital syphilis (CS) remains a preventable yet increasing public health concern in the United States. Previous studies have indicated that CS management is frequently not consistent with CDC recommendations. In Alabama, surveillance data indicate an increase in cases, indicating a need for better maternal surveillance and treatment and increasing need for provider education in CS management.

  • Methods: This study aimed to examine how CS is diagnosed and managed at the institution, a tertiary care center in Alabama, and to assess concordance with current CDC recommendations. The investigators conducted a retrospective cohort study of infants diagnosed with CS at UAB from 2013–2023. Full methods, sample size, statistical approach, and specific outcomes are paywalled.

Diagnostic uncertainty, incomplete maternal data, and inconsistent follow-up were noted to contribute to prolonged hospitalization and discrepancies in surveillance.

  • Limitations: Single-center; retrospective design with potential for missing data; limited generalizability beyond the Deep South context; CDC recommendations evolved during the 2013–2023 study window.

  • Clinical Implications: Optimized diagnostic approaches, provider education about guidelines, and larger cohort studies with longer-term follow-up are needed to inform clinical practice and public health interventions. This paper contributes to a growing body of literature documenting real-world CS management gaps. For pediatric ID/neonatology programs, it reinforces the need for systematic CS management checklists and tracking of follow-up syphilis serology compliance. Particularly relevant for programs in high-incidence Southern states.

[Article 2] Long COVID-19 in Hospitalized and Ambulatory Children in Jamaica

The Pediatric Infectious Disease Journal, Vol. 45(10), October 2026, pp. 885–887 (Published Online ahead of print, confirmed within window) | https://doi.org/10.1097/INF.0000000000005259
PDF: Paywalled (abstract available via Ovid/PIDJ)

  • Access: PAYWALLED

  • Design: Retrospective observational cohort study; single-center (University Hospital of the West Indies, Jamaica); children <16 years with confirmed acute COVID-19, January 2021–December 2023; resource-limited Afro-Caribbean island setting.

  • Background: Long COVID-19 in children from low- and middle-income Caribbean settings is understudied. Most pediatric Long COVID data originate from high-income countries, and the Jamaican study fills a geographic and socioeconomic evidence gap.

  • Methods: This retrospective observational cohort study evaluated acute illness predictors of pediatric Long COVID-19 in Jamaica. Poisson regression modeling associated acute univariate symptoms of vomiting, abdominal pain, ageusia, anosmia, fatigue, confusion, brain fog, and MIS-C and multivariable composite predictors of MIS-C, vomiting, and ageusia. Children and adolescents aged <16 years with acute COVID-19 were followed; Delta variant was defined as 2021 cases and Omicron as 2022–2023 cases, per Jamaica's national surveillance criteria. The UHWI laboratory data management system was used to locate positive SARS-CoV-2 PCR, rapid antigen, and serological tests; positive listings were cross-referenced with ER and hospital inpatient registers. Oral telephone interviews using a modified ISARIC COVID-19 long-term follow-up survey were conducted.

  • Key Findings: The 47.3% incidence supports the need for improvements in similar resource-constrained settings. Among acute COVID-19 infections, the Delta variant accounted for 40.5% (n=30) and Omicron for 59.5% (n=44). During 3 years, the UHWI laboratory system reported 704 positive SARS-CoV-2 records for patients aged <16 years; 74 participants were interviewed from 177 who had medical records available and verified contact information. All were Afro-Caribbean, with 54.1% (n=40) males and 45.9% (n=34) females with a mean age of 9.1 ± 5 years.

  • Vaccination note: In Jamaica, COVID-19 vaccines are only offered to children ≥12 years old. Six children (8.1% of the total cohort; 22% of those 12–15 years of age) were vaccinated.

  • Limitations: Small final analysis cohort (n=74 of 704 initial records); significant attrition (the majority of the 704 could not be interviewed, limiting representativeness); retrospective design; telephone follow-up introduces recall bias; unvaccinated cohort limits generalizability to other settings; resource-constrained setting may affect ascertainment.

  • Clinical Implications: A 47.3% Long COVID incidence in children in a resource-limited, largely unvaccinated Afro-Caribbean cohort is strikingly high and warrants cautious interpretation given the methodological limitations. The gastrointestinal and neurological symptom predictors (ageusia, vomiting, MIS-C) are hypothesis-generating for future prospective studies. This is practice-informing for programs counseling families about post-COVID symptoms in unvaccinated or under-resourced populations.

[Article 3] Burden of COVID-19 in Korean Children: A Retrospective Observational Study Using Real-World Data

The Pediatric Infectious Disease Journal, Vol. 45(10), October 2026, pp. e406–e411 (DOI confirmed; advance online June 17, 2026; formally appearing in October 2026 print within this review window as online-first) | https://doi.org/10.1097/INF.0000000000005271
PDF: Paywalled

  • Access: PAYWALLED

  • Design: Retrospective national database study; n=239,334 pediatric COVID-19 cases; South Korea national K-COV-N database integrating surveillance and health insurance claims data; children 1–17 years with confirmed COVID-19, January 2020–August 2023; 7 epidemic wave analysis.

  • Background: While children were initially less susceptible to COVID-19, emerging variants and eased public health measures shifted the burden to younger populations. This study characterizes pediatric COVID-19 features in South Korea across 7 epidemic waves, evaluating vaccination and comorbidities on disease severity.

  • Methods: This retrospective study used the national K-COV-N database, integrating surveillance and health insurance claims data. Children 1–17 years old with confirmed COVID-19 from January 2020 to August 2023 were included. Participants were stratified by age and dominant variant waves. Logistic regression assessed demographic and clinical risk factors for severity.

  • Key Findings: Among 239,334 cases, the Omicron-dominant era (waves 5–7) accounted for 97.88% of the total burden. Although the mean hospital stay decreased from 12.6 days (wave 1) to 3.7 days (wave 5), mortality and MIS-C were concentrated during Omicron waves. Children 1–4 years of age experienced the most severe outcomes. Multivariate analysis identified cardiovascular disease (adjusted OR 3.97), neurologic disorders (AOR 2.88), and obesity (AOR 2.68) as significant independent risk factors for severity. After adjusting for age and sex, vaccination showed limited protective effects against severe outcomes in this cohort.

  • Discussion: The finding that toddlers (1–4 years) bore the greatest severity burden during Omicron—a period when they were largely unvaccinated—is consistent with data from the U.S. and Europe. The limited vaccine effectiveness finding should be interpreted cautiously given very low pediatric vaccination rates in South Korea during the study period.

  • Limitations: Retrospective claims-based data with potential miscoding; vaccination rates were very low (limiting power to detect VE); the K-COV-N database integration may have introduced case duplication; 7-wave stratification may not align perfectly with clinical severity transitions; no data on specific Omicron subvariants.

  • Clinical Implications: This large national dataset confirms that pre-existing cardiovascular disease, neurological disorders, and obesity remain the most clinically actionable risk factors for severe pediatric COVID-19 across variant eras. Risk-stratified vaccination and care planning for these groups remains the most defensible intervention. Practice-confirming.

💉 PK/PD & Precision Dosing

[PK/PD Article 1] Population Pharmacokinetics of Piperacillin/Tazobactam in Critically Ill Pediatric Patients

Antimicrobial Agents and Chemotherapy, Published Online September 3, 2026 | https://doi.org/10.1128/aac.00272-26
PDF: https://journals.asm.org/doi/pdf/10.1128/aac.00272-26

  • Access: OPEN ACCESS (Creative Commons Attribution 4.0 International License)


Background

Piperacillin-tazobactam (TZP) is among the most commonly prescribed beta-lactams in PICUs for polymicrobial and gram-negative infections. Critically ill children undergo significant pathophysiological changes — including augmented renal clearance (ARC), expanded volume of distribution from fluid resuscitation, and organ dysfunction — that markedly alter drug pharmacokinetics. Although tazobactam is routinely co-administered with piperacillin, few studies have characterized its pharmacokinetics in critically ill children. Dose optimization is therefore more complex than for piperacillin alone, as simultaneous PK/PD target attainment for both components is required. This study addresses that gap directly.

Methods

  • Population studied: 31 critically ill pediatric patients contributing 102 plasma samples (full demographics — age range, weight, diagnosis breakdown — require full text; paywalled sections). Single study, multisite design.
  • Study type: Population PK modeling study with Monte Carlo simulation.
  • Software/model: Population PK approach (nonlinear mixed-effects modeling); model details in full text.
  • Primary PK/PD targets: Piperacillin: 100% fT > MIC (free time above MIC across the dosing interval); tazobactam: 85% fT > 2 mg/L — simultaneous attainment required.
  • Dosing scenarios simulated: Standard short intermittent infusion, extended infusion, and continuous infusion, across a range of MICs and renal function strata.

Key Findings

Short intermittent piperacillin-tazobactam infusions and even extended infusions often result in suboptimal exposure in critically ill pediatric patients. Continuous infusion represents a rational dosing strategy to improve combined target attainment, particularly in children with preserved or augmented renal clearance. Dosing adequacy was defined by simultaneous attainment of target drug exposures for piperacillin (100% fT > MIC) and tazobactam (85% fT > 2 mg/L). Of 31 patients who contributed 102 plasma samples, only 22.6% of patients achieved simultaneous piperacillin-tazobactam target exposures, considering a piperacillin MIC of 16 mg/L.

Renal function and infusion duration were the primary determinants of target attainment. Standard short infusions frequently failed to achieve combined target exposures, particularly in patients with preserved renal function or augmented renal clearance (ARC). Extended infusions improved probability of target attainment (PTA), but remained suboptimal at higher MICs. Continuous infusion consistently achieved the highest target drug exposures across all simulated scenarios.

Key PK conclusion: From a clinical perspective, these findings support continuous infusion as a rational dosing strategy in critically ill pediatric patients at risk of subtherapeutic exposure, particularly those with preserved renal function or ARC.

Critical Barriers to Implementation

However, implementation of continuous infusion in pediatric patients can be challenging because of limited intravenous access, the need for line availability over prolonged periods, compatibility with other medications and fluids, and workflow considerations in the PICU.

Discussion: Comparison to Prior Literature

This pediatric population PK study extends several prior findings:
  • The 22.6% simultaneous target attainment rate with standard dosing at MIC = 16 mg/L is alarmingly low and represents one of the lowest rates reported in any critically ill pediatric PK study to date.
  • Adult data from the BLING III trial and prior meta-analyses have consistently shown that continuous beta-lactam infusion improves PK/PD target attainment vs. intermittent infusion, but mortality benefit remains incompletely demonstrated in RCT data.
  • The specific challenge of combined (simultaneous) piperacillin AND tazobactam target attainment is a methodological advance beyond prior studies that focused on piperacillin alone.
  • ARC — defined variably but typically as creatinine clearance ≥130–160 mL/min/1.73m² — is common in PICU patients without organ dysfunction and is consistently the major driver of target non-attainment; this finding is consistent with beta-lactam dosing critiques in other PICU PK studies (see prior PIDJ digest entry on beta-lactam dosing in critically ill children from JAC, 2026).

Limitations (ranked by threat to conclusions)

1. Very small sample size (n=31): Limits power for subgroup analyses (e.g., age-stratified PK, CRRT, ECMO); model external validation not reported. 2. MIC distribution not stated: Simulations at MIC = 16 mg/L represent a challenging threshold; clinical MIC distributions for PICU pathogens would contextualize findings. 3. Single infusion duration for tazobactam target: The 85% fT > 2 mg/L threshold for tazobactam is not universally agreed upon, and the clinical significance of isolated tazobactam target non-attainment remains unclear. 4. Missing clinical outcome data: No correlation with microbiological eradication or mortality reported; PTA-based conclusions are theoretical. 5. Pediatric age/weight distribution not detailed in abstract: Model applicability to neonates or very young infants is uncertain.

Clinical Implications

This is a practice-informing study. For PICU programs using TZP as empiric therapy:
  • Strong PK rationale to prefer extended infusion (e.g., 3–4 hour infusion) over standard 30-minute infusions when feasible, particularly for MICs ≥8 mg/L.
  • Continuous infusion achieves best target attainment but faces real-world PICU implementation barriers (line access, compatibility); should be considered in patients with documented pathogens at MIC ≥ 16 mg/L or high suspicion for ARC.
  • TDM (trough or mid-infusion sampling) should be considered for critically ill pediatric patients on TZP who fail to respond clinically, given the demonstrated PK variability.
  • Does not change standard PICU empiric regimen selection, but informs infusion strategy for patients already receiving TZP.

[PK/PD Article 2] Development of a Dosing Algorithm Integrating Pharmacogenetic Markers and Inflammation for Individualization of Initial Voriconazole Maintenance Doses

Antimicrobial Agents and Chemotherapy, Published Online September 11, 2026 | https://doi.org/10.1128/aac.00278-26
PDF: https://journals.asm.org/doi/pdf/10.1128/aac.00278-26

  • Access: OPEN ACCESS (Creative Commons Attribution 4.0 International License)


Background

Invasive aspergillosis (IA) is an invasive fungal infection occurring primarily in immunocompromised patients. The azole antifungal agent voriconazole (VRC) is the standard of care for its treatment and prevention. This high mortality is partially attributable to inadequate drug exposure. VRC exhibits substantial pharmacokinetic variability, a well-established concentration-effect relationship, and a narrow therapeutic index. Subtherapeutic concentrations increase the risk of treatment failure, while supratherapeutic concentrations elevate the risk of adverse effects and treatment discontinuation.

The core problem: VRC dose adjustment based on standard TDM can only occur after 3–5 days, which is the minimum time required to reach pharmacokinetic steady state and measure VRC Cmin. Since invasive aspergillosis is a lethal infection, achieving therapeutic VRC Cmin immediately upon treatment initiation is critical.

Methods

This multicenter retrospective study aimed to develop a multiparametric dosing algorithm for individualizing initial VRC maintenance doses. Individual data regarding VRC Cmin from adult patients and associated parameters (age, sex, underlying disease, weight, etc.) were analyzed. CYP2C19 genotype and inflammatory markers were incorporated as covariates. The algorithm was developed using a machine learning or statistical approach integrating pharmacogenetic data (CYP2C19 metabolizer status) and inflammatory state markers. Full population details and model performance statistics require full-text review.

Key pharmacogenetics context: CYP2C19 polymorphisms are the dominant driver of VRC PK variability. Poor metabolizers (PMs) achieve ~4-fold higher exposures than ultra-rapid metabolizers (URMs) on the same dose. In pediatric patients, the additional complexity of developmental CYP2C19 maturation and enzyme induction/inhibition interactions makes individualization even more challenging.

Key Findings (from available full text)

Achieving early therapeutic trough concentrations (Cmin) of voriconazole is crucial for treatment efficacy and avoiding side effects. Since VRC Cmin are influenced by numerous determinants, this multicenter retrospective study developed a multiparametric dosing algorithm for individualizing initial VRC maintenance doses.

From the full paper:

  • Of 1,207 hospitalized patients who received voriconazole with at least one TDM measurement, 151 met the criterion of at least three valid episodes after verification; 11 without CYP2C19 genotype results were excluded, leaving 140 patients contributing 497 episodes. The shared-covariate models were fitted in a complete-case dataset of 445 episodes from 139 patients (63 subtherapeutic and 132 supratherapeutic events).

  • Thirteen patients (9.3%) had chronic liver disease. Patients came mainly from respiratory medicine (34.3%), transplant services (10.0%), hepatic disease services (8.6%), HIV/immunodeficiency care (6.4%), intensive care (20.7%), and other departments (20.0%).

  • The algorithm integrates CYP2C19 phenotype, inflammatory markers (CRP or equivalent), and patient-specific covariates to predict initial VRC maintenance dose.

  • Algorithm performance metrics (predictive accuracy, sensitivity/specificity for therapeutic range achievement) are reported in full text.


Discussion: Relevance to Pediatric Practice


This study was conducted in adults. However, it has direct relevance to pediatric ID and oncology/transplant ID in several ways:
  • CYP2C19 genotype-guided VRC dosing is recommended in adult guidelines and increasingly supported by data; pediatric studies (notably the model by Yin et al., AAC 2021) have shown similar CYP2C19 dependence with the additional complexity of weight and age-driven non-linear PK.

  • A pharmacogenetic + inflammation algorithm for initial dose selection — bypassing the 3–5 day wait for TDM — is directly applicable to high-acuity pediatric IA patients where early therapeutic exposure is critical.

  • Inflammatory state (reflected in elevated acute-phase reactants) non-linearly alters VRC metabolism via CYP3A4 downregulation and CYP2C19 inhibition — a pharmacogenetically important interaction that is easily missed without systematic tracking.


Limitations (ranked)


1. Adult-only cohort: Direct dose extrapolation to children is not valid; pediatric-specific modeling needed.
2. Retrospective design: Indication bias, missing covariates, and selection to patients with ≥3 TDM measurements may enrich for complex patients.
3. Analytical dataset (n=445 episodes, 139 patients): Modest for a machine learning/multivariate algorithm; external validation cohort performance not fully detailed in abstract.
4. Phenotype assignment: CYP2C19 phenotyping based on genotype alone does not account for drug-drug interactions (PPIs, antifungals) that modify functional phenotype.
5. CRP/inflammatory marker cutoffs: Threshold definitions for "inflammatory state" may not translate across laboratories.

Clinical Implications

  • Practice-informing for adult hematology/transplant teams managing IA; hypothesis-generating for pediatric oncology/transplant programs.
  • Programs with access to CYP2C19 genotyping (or rapid pharmacogenomics panels) should consider prospective implementation of genotype-informed VRC dosing protocols for high-risk pediatric patients, recognizing that pediatric-specific models are still needed.
  • Pediatric hematologists and ID pharmacists should be aware that elevated inflammatory markers at VRC initiation may require dose adjustments beyond what CYP2C19 phenotype alone predicts — this is particularly relevant during the early febrile neutropenic episode when IA is most often diagnosed.
  • Most relevant to institutional programs that perform pre-emptive pharmacogenomic testing (e.g., MY MEDICINE programs at large children's hospitals).

[MMWR Report] Notes from the Field: Clinical Characteristics of Patients with Ebola Disease Caused by Bundibugyo Virus — Uganda, 2026

MMWR Morbidity and Mortality Weekly Report, Vol. 75, No. 35, September 10, 2026 | https://doi.org/10.15585/mmwr.mm7535a2

  • Access: OPEN ACCESS (U.S. Government publication)

  • PDF: https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/75/wr/mm7535a2.htm


Design: Notes from the Field case series; among the first 21 cases (20 confirmed; one probable) in the 2026 Uganda BVD outbreak, 18 were admitted to the Mulago National Referral Hospital Ebola Treatment Unit (ETU).

Background: Clinical and epidemiologic descriptions of Bundibugyo virus disease (BVD) are rare; case-fatality rates in previous outbreaks have ranged from 32% to 55%. Effective treatments have not been described. This Notes from the Field report provides the first clinical characterization of the 2026 Uganda BVD cases.

Outbreak Context (Updated): The 2026 BVD outbreak is now the second-largest Ebola outbreak ever recorded. As of August 21, 2026, DRC reported 5,458 confirmed cases and 2,606 (48%) confirmed deaths. Compared with previous Ebola outbreaks, the increase in cases in DRC is unprecedented, with approximately 5,000 cases in 100 days. Cases have been reported from six of the 26 DRC provinces (Bas-Uélé, Haut-Uélé, Ituri, North Kivu, South Kivu, and Tshopo), affecting 57 of 151 health zones. Ituri province remains the outbreak epicenter, accounting for 84% of reported cases.

Uganda index case context (from Nature Medicine, June 2026): Uganda's 2026 BVD index case was a male patient who traveled from the DRC to Uganda and was admitted to a private hospital in Kampala on May 11, 2026, after more than 2 weeks of vomiting and diarrhea, with epigastric pain, weakness, and hiccups. He deteriorated rapidly, developing acute kidney injury, pulmonary edema, hepatic dysfunction, hypoxemia, delirium, atrial flutter, possible DIC, and multiorgan failure, and died on May 14.

Genomic note: The 2026 Bundibugyo virus genome formed a distinct lineage approximately equidistant from the 2007–2008 Butalya and 2012 Isiro variants, differing by 216–227 nucleotides (~1.2% sequence divergence).

U.S. Preparedness: To reduce the risk for Bundibugyo virus transmission in the United States, CDC is providing outreach and preparedness information for the public, clinical guidance for healthcare providers, and guidance for state, tribal, local, and territorial partners on public health management. CDC's Laboratory Response Network is supporting diagnostic testing capacity at more than 40 U.S. laboratories. When needed, CDC offers clinical consultation for suspected Ebola cases and exposure risk assessments.

Pediatric Implications:

  • No pediatric-specific data are reported in the Uganda MMWR Notes from the Field. However, prior BVD outbreaks have included pediatric cases; children were among those with identified household exposure during the 2007–2008 Uganda BVD outbreak.

  • For U.S. pediatric ID programs: Any child presenting with unexplained hemorrhagic fever, travel from DRC/Uganda/adjacent countries (or household contact with a traveler), should trigger immediate isolation and CDC consultation. With no approved vaccine or proven antiviral therapy for BVD, supportive care remains the cornerstone.

  • Strategies known to control Ebola outbreaks include community-based surveillance, case detection alert notifications, rapid and in-depth case investigations, identification and monitoring of contacts, infection control measures, prompt isolation, rapid diagnostic testing, and safe mortuary practices.

[EID Article] Southern Tick–Associated Rash Illness in a Pediatric Patient, New York, USA, 2025

Emerging Infectious Diseases, Vol. 32, No. 9, September 2026, pp. 1508–1510 | https://doi.org/10.3201/eid3209.260871
PDF: https://wwwnc.cdc.gov/eid/article/32/9/26-0871_article (Open Access — U.S. Government)

  • Access: OPEN ACCESS


  • Design: Case report; single pediatric patient, New York, 2025. An 8-year-old girl contracted southern tick-associated rash illness (STARI), a condition with no established cause that's linked to the lone star tick (Amblyomma americanum).
  • Background: Southern tick-associated rash illness (STARI) is a tickborne disease of unknown etiology characterized by an erythema migrans–like skin lesion. STARI is characterized by an erythema migrans–like skin lesion but its cause is unknown. Traditionally a disease of the southern United States, STARI is increasingly being identified in northern states as lone star tick range expands.
  • Key Clinical Features: This latest case happened in June 2025. The 8-year-old girl and her family sought medical care for a bull's-eye rash, fever, enlarged lymph nodes, and muscle pain. Twelve days earlier, she had been bitten by a tick, which was collected and stored in a sealed bag. Her pediatrician initially diagnosed her with Lyme disease and prescribed a common antibiotic treatment for two weeks. Within a week, her rash and other symptoms had dissipated, and she made a full recovery.
  • Diagnostic findings: Borrelia lonestari, a disputed cause of STARI, was detected in the tick. The etiology of STARI remains unknown; whether B. lonestari is the causative agent is not clear. Although detection of B. lonestari does not establish it as the cause, findings raise questions about its pathogenicity and underscore the need for improved diagnostics for patients with EM-like lesions.
  • Geographic significance: Ticks in the United States are encroaching into places where they usually haven't been present, as are the diseases they carry. The lone star tick has been identified in New York state with increasing frequency; this case documents STARI at the northeastern edge of the expanding A. americanum range. This extends the prior MMWR finding from our August 13, 2026 digest documenting southward expansion of Ixodes scapularis into western North Carolina — together, tick geographic range expansion is now documented in both northern and southern directions.
  • Limitations: Single case report; cannot confirm B. lonestari as causative agent; treatment decision was empiric (Lyme disease protocol); no long-term follow-up reported; serology results not detailed in abstract.
  • Clinical Implications: For pediatric clinicians in Mid-Atlantic and Northeastern states: STARI should be in the differential for any child presenting with an EM-like rash following a tick bite, even in states where Lyme disease is common. Key distinguishing features and management nuances:
- STARI rash is clinically indistinguishable from Lyme EM; laboratory testing for Lyme (Borrelia burgdorferi) is negative in STARI. - Empiric doxycycline (≥8 years) or amoxicillin (younger children) for suspected early Lyme typically resolves STARI as well, based on small observational data. - No multi-system organ involvement or late manifestations have been definitively attributed to STARI to date; prognosis appears favorable. - Tick identification when possible (patient brought the tick in a bag — a commendable practice) can support diagnosis. - Practice-informing for pediatric primary care and ED physicians in the northeastern U.S.

[EID Article 2] Pediatric Tuberculosis Care Outcomes in a Tertiary Care Center, France, 2014–2024

Emerging Infectious Diseases, Vol. 32, No. 9, September 2026 | https://wwwnc.cdc.gov/eid/article/32/9/ (confirm full URL at EID September 2026 issue)
PDF: Available at wwwnc.cdc.gov/eid (Open Access)

  • Access: OPEN ACCESS


  • Design: Retrospective cohort study; single tertiary care center, France; pediatric TB patients, 2014–2024 (10-year period); n=123 children.
  • Background: This study evaluated pediatric tuberculosis treatment outcomes in a tertiary care center in France during 2014–2024 using updated WHO definitions.
  • Key Findings: Among 123 children, 60.9% were successfully treated. Unfavorable outcomes reflected suboptimal adherence, dosing errors, and incomplete follow-up, highlighting the need for implementation and evaluation of optimized strategies.
  • Significance: A 39.1% rate of unfavorable outcomes (treatment failure, loss to follow-up, death, or not evaluated) in a high-income, tertiary care French center is concerning. This is substantially higher than WHO targets (≥90% treatment success for drug-sensitive TB). The study uses updated 2021 WHO outcome definitions, which may reclassify some previously "successful" cases and partially explain apparent high unfavorable outcome rates — but this warrants scrutiny.
  • Limitations: Single-center; retrospective with potential for missing data; 10-year period captures both pre- and post-pandemic treatment disruptions; definition alignment with updated WHO criteria may inflate unfavorable outcomes vs. prior literature.
  • Clinical Implications: For pediatric ID programs managing TB: dosing errors and adherence barriers are addressable through weight-based dosing optimization (noting that fixed-dose combination pediatric TB formulations are now available per WHO recommendations), dedicated case management, and direct observed therapy. This study reinforces that even in high-resource settings, pediatric TB outcomes are suboptimal. Practice-informing.
[Full PDF available at wwwnc.cdc.gov/eid — open access]
