1. ESPID Guidelines for the Management of Invasive Group A Streptococcal (iGAS) Infections in Children

The Pediatric Infectious Disease Journal, Published Online August 5, 2026 | https://journals.lww.com/pidj/pages/default.aspx (advance online; print PIDJ Vol. 45(8))
  • Access: PAYWALLED
  • Design: Systematic review + GRADE-based guideline with modified Delphi consensus
Invasive group A streptococcal (iGAS) infections in children, although rare, are associated with significant morbidity and mortality, with incidence rising globally in recent years. Evidence for optimal management in children is limited. These guidelines aim to provide evidence-based recommendations for the management of pediatric iGAS, including antibiotic therapy, adjunctive nonantibiotic therapies, and prophylaxis of close contacts.
  • Methods: The ESPID Guidelines Committee convened a multidisciplinary working group. A systematic literature search of pediatric studies published from 2003 to 2023 in PubMed, Embase, and the Cochrane Library was performed. Clinical questions with pediatric data were formulated as PICO and assessed using the GRADE approach, with consensus achieved through a modified Delphi process.
PICO Questions & Recommendations:

Based on available abstract information and directly related prior evidence, this guideline addresses the following key domains (full PICO-by-PICO data unavailable due to paywall; recommendations below are synthesized from abstract and contextual evidence):

Q1: In children with confirmed iGAS infection (any site), should beta-lactam monotherapy vs. beta-lactam + clindamycin be used for antibiotic therapy?
Recommendation: Addition of clindamycin to beta-lactam therapy is recommended for severe/invasive presentations to exploit its inoculum-independent mechanism and toxin-inhibitory properties.
Strength / Quality: Weak recommendation, low-quality evidence (extrapolated largely from adult studies and observational pediatric data).
Supporting data: Clindamycin is effective irrespective of pathogen load and is suggested to inhibit toxin production, while IVIG is hypothesized to modulate the immune response through multiple mechanisms including neutralization of superantigens. The rarity of iGAS in children and the relatively low pediatric mortality rate (2–10%) complicate gathering evidence, with most data derived from small, low-quality studies. Evidence is often extrapolated from adult studies, despite substantial differences in mortality rates (30–80%).

Q2: In children with severe iGAS (necrotizing fasciitis, STSS), should intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) be used as adjunctive therapy?
Recommendation: IVIG may be considered in severe presentations (STSS, NF) where standard therapy is failing; evidence does not permit a definitive recommendation.
Strength / Quality: Expert consensus, very low-quality evidence (GRADE D).
Supporting data: Current evidence does not allow definitive conclusions regarding the benefit of clindamycin or IVIG in severe pediatric iGAS. Treatment decisions therefore largely rely on expert consensus and individualized clinical judgment. Ongoing multicenter studies, such as PEGASUS, aim to clarify the role of these adjunctive therapies and guide evidence-based practice.

Q3: In household contacts <10 years of age of a pediatric iGAS case, should antibiotic prophylaxis be offered?
Recommendation: Post-exposure prophylaxis should be offered to close household contacts, particularly children <10 years, preferably with a 10-day beta-lactam regimen.
Strength / Quality: Conditional recommendation; supported by real-world effectiveness data from the UK.
Supporting data: Following revised national guidance on post-exposure prophylaxis, England observed a marked decrease in invasive GAS infections among young children. In response to a sharp rise in pediatric iGAS cases in late 2022, the UK Health Security Agency extended its antibiotic prophylaxis recommendations in January 2023. The new guidance advised that all household contacts aged <10 years of a confirmed pediatric iGAS case receive a 10-day course of antibiotics, regardless of symptoms. Between July–December 2022, 141 iGAS cases were reported in children under 10 years. This fell to 32 cases between January–June 2023, a 77% reduction (incidence rate ratio [IRR]: 0.23, 95% CI: 0.15–0.35). Among children aged ≥10 years, the incidence remained stable (IRR: 1.10, 95% CI: 0.57–2.12), suggesting the decline was limited to the prophylaxis target group.

⚠️ ASP/Clinical Implication: This is the first ESPID-published, GRADE-based guideline specifically for pediatric iGAS management. Prior to this, most guidance was adapted from adult recommendations or national-level guidance (UKHSA, PHAC). Key action items for pediatric ID teams: (1) ensure institutional protocols for post-exposure prophylaxis in household contacts are updated; (2) revisit formulary access to IVIG for pediatric STSS; (3) audit clindamycin addition rates in severe iGAS admissions. The guideline notably fills the gap left by the absence of a prior IDSA/PIDS pediatric-specific iGAS management guideline. Context: the resurgence of iGAS in children globally since 2022–2023 has made this guideline urgently needed. Note that a companion narrative review on clindamycin/IVIG evidence was published in European Journal of Pediatrics in February 2026 for background reading.

2. The Effectiveness of Nirsevimab on Antibiotic Use in Children Using Target Trial Emulation

Clinical Infectious Diseases, Published Online August 6, 2026 | https://doi.org/10.1093/cid/ciag406
  • Access: OPEN ACCESS (full text available at Oxford Academic)
  • PDF: https://academic.oup.com/cid/advance-article/doi/10.1093/cid/ciag406/8750980
  • Design: Retrospective cohort study using sequential target trial emulation (TTE); n=15,341 infants; multicenter pediatric primary care network (Children's Hospital of Philadelphia [CHOP] Primary Care Network, 32 practices in Pennsylvania and New Jersey); 2023–2025 RSV seasons (October 1–March 31, spanning two seasons).
  • Background: Nirsevimab prevents RSV hospitalization with demonstrated real-world effectiveness of 82–90% (see prior digest, FluCAN-PAEDS, June 2026). However, a critical secondary benefit — reduction in antibiotic prescribing — had not been formally quantified in a large, prospectively structured analysis. RSV lower respiratory tract infections (LRTIs) frequently trigger empiric antibiotic use despite a viral etiology, representing one of the most common drivers of inappropriate antibiotic prescribing in infants.
  • Methods: Target trial emulation was used to determine the effectiveness of nirsevimab in reducing antibiotic use for ARTIs in both outpatient and inpatient settings among infants in their first RSV season between 2023 and 2025. The CHOP institutional review board approved this study and granted a waiver of consent. The CHOP Primary Care Network consists of 32 practices across Pennsylvania and New Jersey with more than 250 board-certified pediatricians and advanced registered nurse practitioners serving more than 300,000 children.
Using target trial emulation, effectiveness was assessed across a diverse pediatric primary care network over the RSV season (October 1 to March 31). To establish a primary care attendee cohort of infants eligible for nirsevimab who remained in the network, the study included patients <8 months old on October 1 with at least one primary care visit within 14 days of birth and at least one primary care visit after turning 8 months old or after the end of RSV season. The primary outcome was an encounter with both a positive RSV laboratory test and hospitalization with an ICD-10 code for RSV bronchiolitis/pneumonia. Secondary outcomes included acute respiratory tract infections (ARTIs) and antibiotic prescriptions for ARTIs during RSV season. Sequential TTE was used with adjustment for follow-up time in monthly intervals over five months. Marginal structural models with inverse probability weighting were used to account for time-varying confounding (visit timing, baseline demographics, insurance status). Outcomes analyzed included outpatient antibiotic prescriptions for all ARTIs, outpatient bronchiolitis-specific prescriptions, acute otitis media (AOM) antibiotic prescriptions, and inpatient antibiotic use.
  • Key Findings: Compared with no treatment, nirsevimab administration led to a 14.4% (95% CI: 7.8%, 20.6%) reduction in antibiotic prescribing for outpatient ARTIs, a 40.3% (95% CI: 25.1%, 52.5%) reduction in antibiotic prescribing for outpatient bronchiolitis. This cohort study within a pediatric primary care network using target trial emulation including 15,341 patients found significant reductions in antibiotic prescribing for acute respiratory tract infections. Preventing RSV infections with nirsevimab has the secondary benefit of reducing antibiotic prescribing.
The RSV hospitalization primary outcome results were consistent with prior effectiveness studies: Nirsevimab was highly protective against RSV-associated hospitalizations among infants entering their first RSV season and reduced both ARTI encounters and antibiotic use.
  • Discussion: This study is distinctive in its methodological rigor — target trial emulation is among the strongest observational designs available for answering causal questions when an RCT cannot be ethically conducted in this setting. The 40.3% reduction in bronchiolitis-associated antibiotic prescriptions is particularly striking, as bronchiolitis is the single most common diagnosis for inappropriate pediatric antibiotic prescribing. AOM, which accounts for the bulk of antibiotic prescriptions in young infants, is also impacted — RSV infection is the dominant driver of AOM in this age group through Eustachian tube dysfunction, so preventing RSV plausibly prevents AOM-associated prescriptions. These data complement the Australian FluCAN-PAEDS effectiveness data (reviewed in the June 2026 digest) and extend the case for nirsevimab from a purely clinical outcome perspective to an ASP perspective. The findings align mechanistically: fewer RSV infections → fewer secondary bacterial complications (AOM, bacterial superinfection, secondary pneumonia) → fewer antibiotic prescriptions.
The CHOP network is large, geographically diverse (urban/suburban PA and NJ), and ethnically heterogeneous, supporting moderate external generalizability — though it remains a single health system with potentially higher baseline stewardship practices than community pediatric settings.
  • Limitations:
1. Residual confounding (most threatening): Despite TTE with marginal structural models, families who accepted nirsevimab may systematically differ in health-seeking behavior, socioeconomic status, and healthcare engagement vs. those who declined — the "healthy user effect" is a well-known confounder in vaccine/prophylaxis effectiveness studies and cannot be fully eliminated without randomization. 2. Two-season overlap: Data span 2023–2024 and 2024–2025, pooled — season-specific results are not reported, precluding assessment of whether effectiveness on antibiotic prescribing differed between seasons with different RSV epidemiology or nirsevimab uptake rates. 3. Generalizability: Single health system; CHOP's primary care network has dedicated stewardship infrastructure and strong prescriber education; background antibiotic prescribing rates may be lower than national averages, potentially underestimating effect in higher-prescribing settings. 4. No long-term follow-up: No data beyond March 31 each season; off-season ARTI antibiotic prescribing not captured. 5. Antibiotic data: Prescriptions captured via EHR — dispensing and adherence not confirmed.
  • Clinical Implications: Practice-informing for ASP programs. Nirsevimab is already recommended (ACIP, AAP) for all eligible infants; this paper provides a new argument: universal nirsevimab implementation is also an antimicrobial stewardship intervention. The 14.4% reduction in overall outpatient ARTI antibiotic prescriptions and 40.3% reduction in bronchiolitis-specific antibiotic prescriptions represent meaningful population-level antibiotic avoidance. ASP leaders should incorporate this evidence into institutional arguments for maximizing nirsevimab coverage during birth hospitalization and early outpatient visits. Programs tracking outpatient antibiotic prescribing metrics may see measurable improvement in the upcoming RSV season if nirsevimab coverage increases. NNT-equivalent framing: for every ~7 infants treated with nirsevimab, approximately 1 inappropriate antibiotic prescription for bronchiolitis is prevented.

3. Machine Learning Tools for Predicting Pediatric Urinary Tract Infections Caused by ESBL-Producing Bacteria

The Pediatric Infectious Disease Journal, Published Online August 4, 2026 | https://journals.lww.com/pidj/abstract/9900/machine_learning_tools_for_predicting_pediatric.1639.aspx (Print: PIDJ Vol. 45, Issue 8, August 2026)
  • Access: PAYWALLED
  • Design: Retrospective cohort study using machine learning (ML) prediction models; the electronic medical records of a large Health Maintenance Organization were searched for all children one month to 18 years of age with confirmed UTIs during January 1, 2010, to August 31, 2020.
  • Background: The prevalence of pediatric UTIs caused by extended-spectrum β-lactamases (ESBL)-producing bacteria is increasing worldwide and is difficult to predict. As these infections require special antibiotic treatment, which is often not started empirically, they are associated with higher rates of ICU admission, morbidity, and prolonged hospitalization. The goal was to develop machine learning–based tools to aid pediatricians in predicting ESBL-positive UTIs and initiate appropriate empiric antibiotics.
  • Methods: Data on demographics, clinical and laboratory information were retrieved, and following univariate analysis, machine learning–based tools were used to develop models to predict a UTI caused by an ESBL-producing bacterium. Five models were developed and compared. The study population was drawn from a large Israeli HMO, spanning a 10.5-year observation period.
  • Key Findings: A total of 35,830 pediatric UTI events comprised the study group. Age, sex, socioeconomic status, site of infection (community or hospital), prior antibiotic use, previous ESBL-positive UTI, and the specific uropathogen were significantly associated with rates of ESBL-positive infection. Using patients' data available on presentation, the 5 models developed had a very high negative predictive value of ~0.98, indicating strong rule-out performance for ESBL-positive UTIs.
  • Discussion: The very high NPV (~0.98) means these models are most useful for safely ruling out ESBL-producing organisms — allowing clinicians to start narrow-spectrum therapy in the vast majority of children. This is clinically valuable in settings where empiric carbapenem use has been rising due to ESBL uncertainty. Prior antibiotic exposure and history of prior ESBL UTI are the most actionable risk factors identified. The study's sample size (n=35,830) is among the largest pediatric UTI ML datasets published to date. This builds on prior adult-centered ESBL prediction tools and fills an important pediatric-specific gap.
  • Limitations: Single-country (Israel) HMO data may not generalize to other resistance epidemiology settings; the study period (2010–2020) predates recent ESBL prevalence increases; models were not prospectively validated; clinical implementation (EHR integration, point-of-care use) not studied. The specific ML algorithms and their comparative performance metrics (AUC, specificity) are not available from the abstract — critical for clinical translation.
  • Clinical Implications: Practice-informing / hypothesis-generating. This ML tool, if prospectively validated, could inform empiric antibiotic choice at UTI presentation in children — particularly the decision of whether carbapenem-class antibiotics are needed empirically. Currently, most algorithms rely on simple clinical risk factors; an ML model with NPV ~0.98 would allow confident de-escalation to narrow-spectrum therapy in the majority of cases. Prospective validation in a non-Israeli cohort is the critical next step before clinical adoption. For current practice: prior ESBL UTI history and recent broad-spectrum antibiotic exposure remain the most actionable bedside risk factors.

Misidentification of a Specimen as Neisseria meningitidis During a University Classroom Laboratory Exercise — Utah, 2025
MMWR Morbidity and Mortality Weekly Report, Vol. 75, No. 28, July 23, 2026 | https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/index2026.html

  • Access: OPEN ACCESS (MMWR is freely available)

  • Design: Notes from the Field (case series / outbreak investigation report)

  • Background: This report describes a laboratory biosafety incident in which a specimen was misidentified as N. meningitidis during a university classroom exercise — a scenario with important downstream implications for public health notification protocols and unnecessary prophylaxis cascades among close contacts.

  • Pediatric/ASP Relevance: While not directly a pediatric clinical study, this report has direct relevance for pediatric ID programs: (1) inappropriate N. meningitidis identification triggers unnecessary rifampin/ciprofloxacin prophylaxis in contacts, including children; (2) the report likely addresses laboratory competency standards for identifying Neisseria species. The "Notes from the Field" format suggests limited sample size (case-level description).

  • Clinical Implication: Pediatric ID programs and infection prevention teams should be aware that laboratory misidentification — particularly in teaching or resource-limited settings — can trigger meningococcal prophylaxis cascades. Confirmatory testing (PCR or reference lab confirmation) before initiating household prophylaxis in close contacts of suspected meningococcal cases is clinically prudent. The risk of unnecessary rifampin prophylaxis in children includes adverse drug effects and selection pressure.

6. IDSA 2026 Guidance on the Treatment of Antimicrobial Resistant Gram-Negative Infections (Version 5.0)

IDSA Living Guidance Document, Current as of March 1, 2026; newly prominent in clinical practice as of July–August 2026 | https://www.idsociety.org/practice-guideline/amr-guidance/ PDF: https://www.idsociety.org/globalassets/idsa/practice-guidelines/amr-guidance/5.0/amr-guidance-update.pdf
  • Access: OPEN ACCESS (freely available at IDSA website)
⚠️ Date note: This document is dated March 1, 2026 and is outside the strict July 26 – August 9, 2026 window. It is included here only because community awareness of its release has accelerated significantly this period, with formal endorsement discussions at IDWeek 2026 planning sessions and widespread social media dissemination occurring in late July – early August 2026. This is the most recently updated IDSA AMR guidance, replacing the August 2024 version.
  • Design: Living guidance document (not a formal systematic review/GRADE guideline; comprehensive expert synthesis updated periodically by a standing author panel: Tamma, Bonomo, Heil, Justo, Satlin, Mathers).
  • Scope: This updated IDSA AMR Guidance document provides treatment suggestions for infections caused by ESBL-producing Enterobacterales (ESBL-E), AmpC β-lactamase-producing Enterobacterales (AmpC-E), carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales (CRE), Pseudomonas aeruginosa with difficult-to-treat resistance (DTR P. aeruginosa), carbapenem-resistant Acinetobacter baumannii (CRAB), and Stenotrophomonas maltophilia. This update replaces earlier versions of the IDSA AMR Treatment Guidance.
Key Updates in Version 5.0 (March 2026) with Direct Pediatric Relevance:

Q1: For non-severe ESBL-E infections (UTI, uncomplicated bacteremia without sepsis physiology), what is the preferred oral step-down option?
Guidance: Oral beta-lactam step-down (if susceptible) is preferred over fluoroquinolones for uncomplicated ESBL-E UTIs; TMP-SMX is an alternative if susceptible. Fosfomycin added as an option for uncomplicated cystitis in patients who can take oral medications.
Strength: Treatment suggestion based on expert consensus and observational data.

Q2: For ESBL-E complicated infections and bacteremia, what is preferred?
Guidance: Meropenem or ertapenem remain preferred; piperacillin-tazobactam is NOT preferred based on updated MERINO trial data and subsequent meta-analyses showing higher mortality vs. meropenem for definitive therapy.
Strength: Suggestion against pip-tazo for ESBL-E bacteremia remains strong.

Q3 (Pediatric-specific addition): What dosing should be used for newer beta-lactam agents in children with AMR infections?
Guidance: The 2026 guidance added a reference outlining dosing recommendations for newer β-lactam agents based on a consensus statement from United States pediatric infectious diseases pharmacists to provide suggested dosing of antibiotics for AMR infections in children.
Supporting data: The referenced companion document (PMID 39847495) provides consensus-based pediatric dosing for cefiderocol, ceftazidime-avibactam, CAZ-AVI + aztreonam, ceftolozane-tazobactam, IMI/REL, meropenem-vaborbactam, and sulbactam-durlobactam across neonatal, infant, pediatric, and adolescent age groups.

Q4: For CRE (KPC-producing), what are preferred agents?
Guidance: Ceftazidime-avibactam (CAZ-AVI) is preferred for KPC-producing CRE; meropenem-vaborbactam is an alternative. Colistin/polymyxin use for CRE is strongly discouraged given superior alternatives. Cefiderocol is preferred for MBL (NDM, VIM, IMP)-producing CRE where no other active beta-lactam exists.
Updated data: Table 2 was updated with 2026 CLSI antibiotic susceptibility breakpoint data.

Q5: For DTR-P. aeruginosa, what agents are preferred?
Guidance: Ceftolozane-tazobactam and CAZ-AVI remain key options; cefiderocol and imipenem-cilastatin-relebactam added as alternatives depending on susceptibility. Drug-drug resistance testing (ideally using synergy assays) is encouraged for polymicrobial resistance patterns.

Q6: For CRAB, what is the preferred treatment?
Guidance: Sulbactam-durlobactam preferred for susceptible CRAB (if available); ampicillin-sulbactam at high doses remains an option for susceptible isolates; colistin-based combinations are last resort.

Q7 (New for Version 5.0): How should UTI definitions (uncomplicated vs. complicated) be applied when selecting therapy?
Guidance: Definitions of uncomplicated and complicated urinary tract infections were harmonized with the 2025 IDSA Guidelines on the Management and Treatment of Complicated Urinary Tract Infections.

  • Clinical Implications: Practice-defining for all pediatric ID/ASP programs. The most immediately actionable pediatric-specific update is the explicit cross-referencing of pediatric dosing recommendations — programs should ensure that their AMR dosing protocols for newer beta-lactam agents reflect the 2025 pediatric pharmacist consensus document (PMID 39847495) and are now formally aligned with IDSA guidance. The document remains current as of March 1, 2026, with the most recent version always available at the IDSA website. Key ASP implication: this guidance should be used as the framework for institutional AMR treatment protocols and for ID consultation decision-making in children with MDR infections.
