Effect of Institutional Guidelines on Equitable Management of Pediatric Osteomyelitis
Journal of the Pediatric Infectious Diseases Society (JPIDS), Published Online: September 24, 2026 | https://doi.org/10.1093/jpids/piag103
PDF: Open access status not confirmed; abstract available at Oxford Academic.

  • Access: PAYWALLED (advance article abstract only reviewed)

  • Design: Retrospective cohort study, single-center (University of Michigan / C.S. Mott Children's Hospital, Ann Arbor, MI). The study evaluated whether institutional AHO guideline implementation improved care delivery and reduced disparities in sociodemographic groups, comparing children aged 90 days to 18 years hospitalized with AHO across pre-guideline (September 1, 2014–July 31, 2017) and post-guideline implementation (November 1, 2017–December 31, 2023) periods.

  • Background: Pediatric AHO is a common and potentially morbid musculoskeletal infection requiring multidisciplinary care. The 2021 PIDS/IDSA AHO guideline recommends early IV-to-oral antibiotic transition and standardized diagnostic workflows. Clinical practice guidelines aim to standardize care and reduce variation, but their impact on health equity — specifically whether they reduce or inadvertently widen sociodemographic disparities — is not well understood. This study fills an important implementation science gap.

  • Methods: Outcomes assessed included blood culture acquisition rates, laboratory and imaging utilization, appropriate empiric antibiotic administration, number of inpatient pediatric ID consultations, and clinical outcomes. Care process measures included blood culture acquisition, laboratory testing, imaging use and timing, bone biopsy, administration of any antibiotics, administration of appropriate empiric antibiotics, and number of inpatient PID consultations. Sociodemographic stratifiers (race/ethnicity, insurance status, language, zip-code based socioeconomic proxies) were used to assess equity. Full regression methods not available from abstract.

  • Key Findings: Implementation of institutional guidelines for pediatric AHO increased appropriate empiric antibiotic use. Full results — including numerical effect sizes, CIs, and equity subgroup data — are behind the paywall and cannot be fully reported.

  • Discussion: This work directly extends the existing evidence base for AHO guideline implementation studies. The question of equity is highly relevant: prior work (e.g., the 2021 PIDS/IDSA AHO guideline itself) explicitly cited equity as a reason to recommend oral transition over OPAT, but data on whether guidelines actually close demographic gaps in process adherence is sparse. This study appears to be one of the first to prospectively examine that question in a U.S. pediatric tertiary center. Readers should also note the concurrent abstract presented at IDWeek 2026 (OFID, ofaf695.644) demonstrating modest real-world uptake of early oral step-down after the 2021 guidelines at another center, with an unexpected increase in piperacillin-tazobactam use.

  • Limitations (from abstract): Single-center, retrospective design; limited generalizability to community or non-tertiary settings. Long post-implementation window (6+ years) may obscure temporal trends or secular confounders (e.g., COVID-19 disruptions). Socioeconomic proxies (zip-code based) are imprecise.

  • Clinical Implications: Practice-informing. For ASP leaders: guideline-concordant care is necessary but not sufficient — equity monitoring should be built into AHO quality improvement programs. Programs should track empiric antibiotic appropriateness stratified by payer status and race/ethnicity to identify persistent gaps post-guideline implementation.

  • Design: Prospective nested cohort study (EARTH cohort), Mozambique (southern region). Published September 28, 2026. This prospective nested cohort (EARTH) study evaluated drug resistance mutations and outcomes among 79 (31 with… — full population denominators not available from abstract.

  • Background: Mozambique bears one of the world's highest HIV burdens. Drug resistance mutation (DRM) rates in pediatric patients on ART are substantially higher than in adults or pregnant women — historical data show pediatric DRM rates of approximately 74.7% in sequenced samples in the ART era, driven largely by NNRTI and NRTI mutations. The country shifted to dolutegravir (DTG)-based first-line regimens in 2022 (pediatric formulation pDTG 10mg + ABC/3TC), but legacy NNRTI/NRTI resistance from prior NVP-based PMTCT programs persists as a major challenge. Specific inclusion criteria, endpoints, and statistical approach not available from abstract.

  • Key Findings: Full results not available from abstract. The study population included mother-infant pairs in southern Mozambique with characterization of DRM profiles in the context of evolving PMTCT regimens.

  • Discussion: This dataset is uniquely positioned to characterize DRM transmission in the post-DTG transition period in Mozambique. Real-world data from the DREAM cohort showed that among adults failing DTG in Mozambique, 46.4% harbored DTG resistance mutations — frequently G118R, E138K, L74M, and R263K — with multiclass resistance common. Whether these patterns appear in mother-infant transmission events in the EARTH cohort will be critical for informing salvage options. Historical NVP-based PMTCT programs in Mozambique conferred DRM rates of 35–85% in sequenced infants, predominantly NNRTI class. This study should provide the first contemporary prospectively-collected paired DRM data in southern Mozambique.

  • Limitations: Single-region cohort limits generalizability across Mozambique (DRM rates differ significantly between Maputo and Beira); Sanger sequencing may miss minority variants.

  • Clinical Implications: Hypothesis-generating for U.S. practitioners caring for internationally-adopted children or refugee children from Mozambique with perinatal HIV exposure. Directly relevant to global pediatric ID programs advising Mozambican PMTCT programs on regimen selection and resistance testing thresholds.

Safety of Rotavirus Vaccination in a Growing Care Unit (Intermediate-care Neonatal Unit) in Japan
The Pediatric Infectious Disease Journal, Vol. 45(10), October 2026, pp. 911–915 | https://doi.org/10.1097/INF.0000000000005251
PDF: Not open access (LWW paywalled).

  • Access: PAYWALLED — abstract fully indexed on PubMed (PMID available); published online April 22, 2026; appearing in October 2026 print issue within this window.

  • Design: Prospective cohort study in a Japanese growing care unit (GCU), a step-down neonatal unit comparable to Level II–III neonatal intensive care units in the United States. Duration: 1 year. n=237 infants (15 vaccinated with 19 doses of RV1; 222 unvaccinated contemporaneous controls).

  • Background: Rotavirus vaccination effectively prevents severe rotavirus gastroenteritis; however, administration during neonatal hospitalization is often avoided because of theoretical concerns regarding vaccine-virus transmission. Data on the safety of in-hospital rotavirus vaccination in neonatal step-down care settings remain limited. Existing evidence supports in-NICU rotavirus vaccination in preterm infants but has been limited to NICU rather than step-down GCU settings.

  • Methods: Hospitalized infants were monitored for adverse events and vaccine-strain shedding after administration of monovalent rotavirus vaccine (RV1). Stool samples were collected weekly and analyzed using RV1 strain-specific real-time quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction targeting the NSP2 gene. Primary outcomes: adverse events (including serious AEs) and evidence of horizontal transmission of vaccine strain RNA.

  • Key Findings: Among 237 infants included in the analysis, 15 received a total of 19 doses of RV1 during hospitalization. RV1 vaccine-strain RNA was detected in 26 of 38 postvaccination stool samples (68.4%). No RV1 strain RNA was detected in unvaccinated infants or in samples collected before vaccination. No serious adverse events were observed, and no evidence of horizontal transmission was identified. Six vaccinated infants exceeded the upper age limit for vaccine initiation at discharge and would have missed vaccination opportunities without in-hospital vaccination.

  • Discussion: RV1 vaccination was not associated with detectable transmission or serious adverse events in a neonatal step-down care setting under routine contact precautions, supporting its potential safety and role in preventing missed vaccination opportunities among high-risk infants. Vaccine-strain shedding was detected in stool (68.4% of post-vaccination samples), which is consistent with prior data from NICU studies but no shedding was detected in adjacent unvaccinated infants. The "missed vaccination" concern is clinically real — 6/15 vaccinated infants would have aged out of eligibility by discharge, a non-trivial proportion. This study adds to prior Italian multicenter NICU data supporting in-hospital RV vaccination in preterm/fragile infants.

  • Limitations: Small number of vaccinated infants (n=15, 19 doses), single center, single country (Japanese growing care unit context may not generalize to all neonatal step-down units). Shedding was tracked by quantitative RT-PCR (NSP2 gene) — absence of PCR signal in adjacent infants does not exclude very low-level exposure; however, no clinical illness was observed. Limited power to detect rare serious AEs.

  • Clinical Implications: Practice-informing. For pediatric ID physicians and neonatologists advising on in-hospital rotavirus vaccination policy in Level II–III step-down settings: this study supports safety under standard contact precautions. The "missed vaccination window" problem is operationally important — GCU/step-down units should have clear protocols for offering RV1 during hospitalization to eligible infants before they exceed the age cutoff (14 weeks 6 days for first dose).

Health and Economic Impact of Seasonal Maternal Vaccination With Bivalent Respiratory Syncytial Virus Prefusion F Vaccine in Belgium
The Pediatric Infectious Disease Journal, Vol. 45(10), October 2026, pp. 905–910 | https://doi.org/10.1097/INF.0000000000005226
PDF: Not open access (LWW paywalled).

  • Access: PAYWALLED — full abstract available at Ovid.

  • Design: Static decision-analytic cost-effectiveness model (health economic modeling study) from the Belgian public healthcare payer perspective. Not a clinical cohort — uses observational inputs from Belgian RSV hospitalization surveillance and published RSVpreF clinical trial data.

  • Background: RSV is the leading cause of potentially severe lower respiratory tract viral infection in infants. Hospitals may encounter challenges during the RSV season following the increased number of pediatric patients' admissions, and maternal vaccination can help address this healthcare threat. Belgium-specific cost-effectiveness data for RSVpreF maternal vaccination were previously lacking.

  • Methods: A static model was developed to assess the impact of RSV-LRTI immunization via maternal vaccination on hospitalization, life expectancy, and quality of life in an annual newborn population. Key input data were derived from an independent Belgian observational study and the latest available clinical data on the bivalent RSVpreF vaccine. Model population: approximately 116,022 pregnant women and 117,884 infants (including stillbirths), based on Belgian regional data (Federal Plan Bureau, year 2025). RSV hospitalization rate inputs: 68.3 per 1,000 infants ages 0–11 months (with highest rates 137.1/1,000 in 0–2 months). Extreme preterm infants (≤27 weeks GA) were excluded from the maternal vaccination modeled cohort.

  • Key Findings: Full numerical outputs (ICERs, QALYs gained, hospitalizations prevented) are paywalled. The study concluded that maternal RSVpreF vaccination reduces RSV-LRTI hospitalization burden and offers favorable cost-effectiveness from the Belgian public payer perspective. Contextualizing with the concurrent published npj Vaccines modeling from Belgium (2026), maternal vaccine + nirsevimab combination strategies confer additional QALY gains and demonstrate return-on-investment metrics favorable versus vaccine alone.

  • Discussion: This model-based study complements the real-world effectiveness data (nirsevimab 80.7% VE, maternal vaccine 70% VE) reviewed in the September 2026 JAMA systematic review (previously reviewed). Belgium's national RSV prophylaxis decision was pending this economic evidence. The study's limitation — static model, single-country, industry-linked inputs — applies to all such analyses. Notably, the Belgian birth distribution input (91.6% full-term, 7.0% late preterm) is substantially preterm-heavy compared to U.S. estimates, which could affect generalizability of absolute hospitalization estimates.

  • Limitations: Static (not dynamic) model; does not capture herd effects or RSV strain circulation changes; industry funding likely (review for COI disclosure); applicable primarily to high-income European healthcare systems. No sensitivity analysis details available from abstract.

  • Clinical Implications: Practice-informing for health policy. For U.S. pediatric ID and hospital medicine programs: the Belgian economic case reinforces the already-established effectiveness case for maternal RSVpreF vaccination. This study does not change the clinical recommendation but provides European cost-effectiveness context relevant to programs advising international health systems. The notable finding that extreme preterm infants (≤27 weeks) are excluded from the maternal vaccine modeled cohort reinforces the continued need for nirsevimab as the primary RSV preventive strategy in this highest-risk group.

Safety and Immunogenicity of Varicella Vaccination in Children and Adolescents Living With HIV
The Pediatric Infectious Disease Journal, Vol. 45(10), October 2026, pp. 916–924 | https://journals.lww.com/pidj (confirm final DOI at journals.lww.com/pidj)
PDF: Not open access.

  • Access: PAYWALLED — current issue highlights available.

  • Design: Systematic review/narrative synthesis of global literature. Not a primary study — summarizes published data on safety and immunogenicity of varicella vaccine in HIV-positive children globally.

  • Background: Preventing varicella-zoster virus infection is important in the management of HIV-positive persons. Varicella remains endemic worldwide, and HIV-positive persons can experience greater morbidity than the general population.

  • Methods: Global literature review; exact search strategy, databases, and date range not available from abstract. Authors summarized the global literature on the safety and immunogenicity of the varicella vaccine in HIV-positive children.

  • Key Findings: A vaccine-related rash was reported in less than 5% of vaccinees, with fewer lesions and shorter duration than typical varicella. Serious adverse events were reported only when the vaccine was inadvertently administered to children with severe HIV immunosuppression. Varicella vaccination had no clinically significant impact on plasma HIV RNA or CD4+ T-cell count after either dose.

  • Discussion: This is a timely synthesis given the global expansion of pediatric HIV treatment and increasing access to antiretroviral therapy. The key finding — that inadvertent vaccination of severely immunocompromised children (not adequately immunoreconstituted) is associated with serious AEs — reinforces current AAP/CDC guidance requiring CD4% threshold confirmation before varicella vaccination in HIV-positive children. The reassuring finding on HIV viremia and CD4 stability post-vaccination supports the practice of vaccinating adequately immunoreconstituted HIV-positive children.

  • Limitations: Systematic review quality depends on the included study heterogeneity; immunosuppression definitions and CD4 thresholds varied across studies. Global literature includes settings with differing HIV clade distribution and ART regimens. Full GRADE quality assessment not reported in abstract.

  • Clinical Implications: Practice-confirming. Varicella vaccination in HIV-positive children with adequate immune reconstitution (CD4% ≥15% or age-appropriate threshold) is safe and immunogenic based on accumulated global evidence. Caution is warranted in children with severe immunosuppression. Pediatric HIV programs should maintain varicella vaccination tracking as part of routine immunization monitoring.

The Pediatric Infectious Disease Journal, Vol. 45(10), October 2026, pp. e420–e426 | https://journals.lww.com/pidj (confirm DOI)
PDF: Not open access.

  • Access: PAYWALLED

  • Design: Case series; n=4 fatal influenza cases in young children (ages 2–6 years) from Southwest Germany. Retrospective case characterization across two influenza seasons (2022/23 and 2023/24).

  • Background: Seasonal influenza virus infections remain a major public health burden. During the last years, several case reports of severe influenza infections in children have been published. Fatal influenza in previously healthy young children is rare but well-documented, and its occurrence even in healthy children without recognized risk factors reinforces the pediatric influenza vaccination debate — particularly in European countries where routine child influenza vaccination is not universally implemented.

  • Key Findings: The case series reports 4 fatal influenza cases in young children (ages 2–6 years) that occurred in Southwest Germany in the 2022/23 and 2023/24 seasons. In one case, influenza A(H1N1)pdm09 could be cultivated in cell culture from brain tissue, indicating CNS neurotropism. This case series underlines the potential risk of fatal outcomes of influenza infections in children.

  • Discussion: The detection of influenza A(H1N1)pdm09 in brain tissue is noteworthy and supports the growing evidence for influenza-associated encephalitis/encephalopathy as a mechanism of fatal influenza in children. The European context is important: unlike the U.S. (which recommends annual influenza vaccination for all children ≥6 months), most European countries lack universal pediatric influenza vaccination programs. This should be considered when re-evaluating vaccination recommendations for healthy children.

  • Limitations: Case series of only 4 patients; highly prone to selection/reporting bias; single region of Germany; no population-level denominator to calculate attack rates or case-fatality rates.

  • Clinical Implications: Practice-informing. For U.S. practitioners: provides European context supporting the rationale for universal pediatric influenza vaccination. For pediatric ID programs advising European colleagues: adds to the advocacy evidence for universal childhood influenza vaccination policy adoption. The CNS tissue culture positivity case is a diagnostic teaching point — in children with influenza + encephalopathy, virus may be recoverable from CNS tissue directly.

Journal of the Pediatric Infectious Diseases Society (JPIDS), Advance Article (listed as Latest Article on JPIDS homepage as of October 2026) | https://academic.oup.com/jpids (confirm DOI upon full publication)
PDF: Advance article — access status to be confirmed at Oxford Academic.

  • Access: Publication date within window confirmed by JPIDS homepage listing; DOI to be confirmed.

  • Design: Study design, sample size, and setting not yet fully confirmed from available abstract. Appears to be a pediatric cohort/diagnostic accuracy study evaluating the BioFire® FilmArray® Pneumonia Panel (BFPP) applied to pleural fluid specimens from children with complicated CAP (parapneumonic effusion/empyema), with pathogen identification and quantification as primary outcomes.

  • Background: Microbiological diagnosis in pediatric complicated pneumonia is limited by low culture yield in pleural fluid — prior studies show culture positivity rates of only 25–40% even with blood culture bottle inoculation. Molecular multiplex PCR panels have demonstrated superior sensitivity (94% in a 17-patient Spanish case series, vs. 25% for culture), with management changes in approximately 52% of PCR-guided cases. The BFPP was FDA-cleared for lower respiratory specimens (BAL, sputum, endotracheal aspirate) but its performance in pleural fluid — a sterile, typically low-volume specimen — is not formally validated.

  • Key Findings: Full results pending DOI confirmation and access. The JPIDS homepage listing confirms this is among the journal's most prominently featured current articles.

  • Discussion: The application of BFPP to pleural fluid fills a critical diagnostic gap in pediatric empyema management. The 2026 IDSA/PIDS CAP guideline update addresses pleural fluid drainage indications but does not yet formally endorse molecular panels for pleural fluid pathogen identification. Prior work using the BIOFIRE Joint Infection Panel (JI Panel) in pleural fluids — including a CID 2026 study (n=162 adult and pediatric specimens) — demonstrated that S. pneumoniae was the most reliably detected organism, but false-positive rates for other organisms were concerning. Pediatric-specific data from this JPIDS study will be particularly informative given the predominance of S. pneumoniae and S. pyogenes in pediatric empyema.

  • Clinical Implications: Potentially practice-informing for centers managing pediatric empyema. If BFPP demonstrates acceptable sensitivity and specificity in pleural fluid, it could enable pathogen-directed therapy within hours of thoracentesis — before culture results — and support antibiotic de-escalation in empyema currently being treated empirically with broad-spectrum agents.

Levofloxacin-Induced Ataxia in a Child After Complicated Pneumonia
The Pediatric Infectious Disease Journal, Published Online: September 29, 2026 | https://journals.lww.com/pidj (confirm DOI at journals.lww.com/pidj)
PDF: Not open access (LWW).

  • Access: PAYWALLED — abstract available on journal homepage.

  • Design: Case report. Single pediatric patient.

  • Background: Levofloxacin is increasingly used in children with complicated CAP — particularly in penicillin allergy, suspected MRSA (in combination), or macrolide-resistant atypical pneumonia — as an off-label agent. Fluoroquinolone-associated CNS adverse events (including seizures, psychosis, and movement disorders) are recognized in adults but sparsely reported in pediatric patients.

  • Key Findings: A 6-year-old child with complicated necrotizing pneumonia developed acute ataxia, urticarial rash, and urinary incontinence on the third day of levofloxacin therapy. No structural abnormalities were found on imaging. The antibiotic was discontinued, and corticosteroids with antihistamines were initiated, leading to complete symptom resolution without sequelae. This pediatric case underscores the need for vigilance regarding levofloxacin-induced movement disorders, even with short-term use.

  • Discussion: Fluoroquinolone CNS toxicity mechanisms include GABA-A receptor antagonism and quinolone-induced inhibition of gamma-aminobutyric acid binding. Movement disorders — including ataxia — are less commonly reported than seizures or psychiatric symptoms. The complete resolution following drug discontinuation and corticosteroid/antihistamine therapy in this case suggests a hypersensitivity-mediated mechanism (consistent with the urticarial rash) rather than pure pharmacodynamic CNS toxicity. The timing (day 3) aligns with the known peak accumulation window. The IDSA/PIDS 2026 CAP guideline continues to list levofloxacin as an alternative in penicillin-allergic children with severe CAP.

  • Limitations: Single case report — inherent publication bias; cannot estimate incidence. Alternative diagnoses (viral encephalitis, para-infectious ataxia from the primary pneumonia itself) cannot be entirely excluded, though temporal resolution with drug discontinuation strongly supports drug causality.

  • Clinical Implications: Practice-informing safety alert. Pediatric ID clinicians and pharmacists should:

1. Counsel families about CNS adverse events (ataxia, agitation, seizures) when initiating levofloxacin in children.
2. Maintain a low threshold for drug discontinuation if acute neurological symptoms develop during fluoroquinolone therapy.
3. Document this event in adverse drug reaction systems (MedWatch or institutional).
4. Consider that the presentation (ataxia + urticarial rash + urinary incontinence) may prompt unnecessary neuroimaging — awareness of this toxidrome can streamline the diagnostic workup.

  • Access: OPEN ACCESS (free at CDC.gov)

  • PDF: https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/75/wr/pdfs/mm7538a2-H.pdf (confirm URL)

  • Design: Notes from the Field (single fatal case report with public health investigation). n=1 confirmed fatal pneumonic plague case.

  • Background: Pneumonic plague is a rare and often fatal disease that can be transmitted by respiratory droplets from animals or persons infected with Yersinia pestis bacteria. Pneumonic plague is transmitted through inhalation of respiratory droplets containing Y. pestis and has a nearly 100% case-fatality rate when untreated. Although Y. pestis is endemic among rodents in northern Arizona, only eight human plague cases were reported in Arizona during 2000–2024.

  • Key Findings: A fatal case of pneumonic plague occurred in Coconino County, Arizona, in July 2025 — the report is published in October 2026 MMWR following investigation completion. Clinical details, contact tracing, and public health response are described. Yersinia pestis misidentification can occur with automated pathogen detection systems, including MALDI-TOF. The combination of Gram stain results, MALDI-TOF results, the patient's clinical signs and symptoms, and known endemicity of Y. pestis should guide laboratory workup.

  • Pediatric Relevance: Pneumonic plague is not pediatric-specific, but Coconino County (northern Arizona/Navajo Nation region) encompasses areas where children play and camp outdoors. Any febrile child with pneumonia and rodent/flea exposure in Arizona or the Southwest warrants consideration of plague. Antibiotic treatment: gentamicin or doxycycline (streptomycin if available) are first-line; doxycycline is appropriate for children ≥8 years. Post-exposure prophylaxis with doxycycline (or trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole for younger children) is indicated for close respiratory contacts.

  • Clinical Implications: For pediatric ID and emergency medicine practitioners in the Western U.S.: maintain a high index of suspicion for plague in children with rapidly progressive pneumonia and rodent or flea exposure. MALDI-TOF can misidentify Y. pestis — contact state health department and CDC immediately if plague is suspected; do not rely solely on automated identification systems. Contact tracing for respiratory contacts of pneumonic plague is a public health emergency.

[MMWR Early Release] Notes from the Field: Molecular Detections of Cladophialophora bantiana at a Clinical Reference Laboratory — United States, 2009–2026
MMWR Morbidity and Mortality Weekly Report, Early Release: October 2, 2026 | https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/75/wr/mm7540e1.htm

  • Access: OPEN ACCESS

  • PDF: Available at CDC.gov (early release, Vol. 75)

  • Design: Notes from the Field — retrospective case series using clinical reference laboratory records (University of Washington clinical reference laboratory). Surveillance period: January 2008–July 2026.

  • Background: Cladophialophora bantiana is a neurotropic fungus; infection is associated with a 60%–70% case-fatality rate. National surveillance does not exist, and neither the environmental reservoir nor the route of infection is fully understood.

  • Key Findings: During January 2008–July 2026, 48 confirmed cases and one probable case of C. bantiana were detected in clinical samples (predominantly central nervous system tissue [88%]) originating in 22 jurisdictions and tested at a single clinical reference laboratory; detections of the fungus increased approximately fourfold from 2023 to 2025.

  • Discussion and Implications: The approximately fourfold increase in detections from 2023–2025 is striking and unexplained. Three hypotheses: (1) increased use of molecular diagnostics (metagenomics/NGS) that improve detection of this otherwise culture-difficult organism; (2) true increase in environmental exposure or population susceptibility; (3) increased awareness and testing prompted by prior case reports. Reasons for the apparent increase are unknown. Further research into epidemiologic risk factors and potentially evolving virulence are warranted. Systematic reporting of C. bantiana cases is needed and C. bantiana should be considered in patients with a brain abscess. Patients with infection require prompt antifungal therapy, and surgical excision might improve patient outcomes.

  • Pediatric Relevance: C. bantiana cerebral phaeohyphomycosis is extraordinarily rare in children but has been reported in pediatric patients — including in immunocompetent hosts. A documented pediatric case (3-year-old in India) was initially misidentified as C. bantiana but confirmed as Fonsecaea monophora — highlighting the importance of molecular confirmation. For pediatric ID: any child with CNS space-occupying lesion where common bacterial, mycobacterial, and cryptococcal etiologies are excluded should prompt consideration of dematiaceous fungi, especially with relevant environmental exposures. Plasma cell-free DNA metagenomic sequencing (e.g., Karius) has demonstrated utility in diagnosing C. bantiana prior to culture positivity.

  • Treatment considerations: Voriconazole is the preferred agent; combination with terbinafine or flucytosine may be considered for severe CNS disease. Surgical excision in accessible lesions combined with prolonged antifungal therapy (12+ months) is the current standard of care. No pediatric-specific dosing data specific to C. bantiana CNS infection are available.

MMWR Early Release: Cladophialophora bantiana — Laboratory Safety Note
C. bantiana should be considered in patients with a brain abscess and CNS samples sent for fungal culture from such patients may represent a laboratory biosafety consideration (BSL-2 with enhanced precautions). Laboratories receiving CNS tissue for culture should be notified of clinical suspicion.
