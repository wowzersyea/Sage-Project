U.S. LOSES MEASLES ELIMINATION STATUS — PAHO DETERMINATION, November 13–14, 2026

This is not a journal article but a landmark public health development with direct clinical implications.

The Pan American Health Organization (PAHO) has revoked the United States' measles elimination status, ending a 26-year run after determining the country failed to halt continuous transmission of the highly contagious virus for more than a year.

An outbreak in Utah that infected more than 700 people met PAHO's 12-month sustained transmission criteria, costing the U.S. its status.

As of November 12, 2026, 5,412 confirmed measles cases were reported in the United States in 2026 — more than double last year's total and the highest number since 1991. Hospitalizations have reached 591, or 11% of all 2026 cases.

The United States is experiencing its worst and deadliest year for measles since 1991, with 10 measles-associated deaths so far in 2026 — half of which were reported in the past two weeks.

The national MMR vaccination rate in school-age children reached a new low of 92.4% in the 2025–2026 school year, with 39 states reporting rates that fall below the 95% target threshold.

The United States can re-establish its measles elimination status once it shows that outbreak transmission has been interrupted for at least 12 months.

Clinical Action Items for Pediatric ID:

  • Reinforce MMR vaccination urgency for all age-eligible unvaccinated or incompletely vaccinated children, including during hospitalizations

  • Maintain high clinical suspicion for measles in febrile rash illness regardless of vaccination status (vaccine failure occurs and cases are occurring in vaccinated individuals in some outbreaks)

  • Ensure your institution's measles exposure response protocol (airborne isolation, rapid public health notification) is current

  • Pre-exposure vitamin A supplementation guidance for high-risk children (malnourished, vitamin A deficient) should be revisited in your high-risk populations

AAP 2026 Clinical Practice Guideline for the Diagnosis and Treatment of Urinary Tract Infection in Children From 8 Days to 5 Years of Age

Pediatrics, Published online September 28, 2026; Formal issue October 2026 (Vol. 158, Issue 4) https://doi.org/10.1542/peds.2026-078565 PDF: Paywalled
Note: This guideline was published in September/October 2026 and was not included in prior digests. It is included here as it reached broad dissemination this period.
  • Access: PAYWALLED (checked: Unpaywall/Europe PMC)
  • Design: Clinical Practice Guideline — multidisciplinary panel, systematic evidence review, first update since 2011
  • Background: The previous update to the AAP clinical practice guideline on the diagnosis and management of UTI was published in 2011 and subsequently reaffirmed in 2016; that CPG is now retired. The previous AAP guideline was retired in 2021 because it improperly used race as a biological factor when calculating disease risk.
PICO Questions & Recommendations:

Q1: In children 8 days to 5 years with fever without source or urinary symptoms, when should UTI be evaluated?
Recommendation: For medically non-complex children older than 2 months with fever without an identified source or urinary symptoms, evaluation for UTI should occur within 72 hours.
Strength / Quality: Strong recommendation

Q2: How should UTI be diagnosed, and what are the laboratory thresholds?
Recommendation: For infants under 2 months, initiate treatment when ≥5 WBC/hpf or ≥trace leukocytes; for infants and children older than 2 months, initiate treatment when ≥10 WBC/hpf or ≥+2 leukocytes. The urine culture threshold for catheterized specimens is ≥10,000 CFU/mL of a pathogenic organism with evidence of inflammation/pyuria.
Strength / Quality: Strong recommendation, Moderate-quality evidence
Supporting data: The urine culture threshold for catheterized specimens has been reduced from 50,000 to 10,000 colony-forming units per milliliter. Evidence reviewed by the panel suggested that this lower threshold increases sensitivity with only a small loss in specificity, potentially allowing earlier identification of true infections in young children.

Q3: What is the preferred route of antibiotic therapy for children >28 days?
Recommendation: In children aged older than 28 days and younger than 5 years, oral antibiotics are recommended instead of parenteral or sequential antibiotics. For children aged 8–28 days, parenteral antibiotics are suggested with early transition to oral following clinical improvement.
Strength / Quality: Strong recommendation

Q4: What is the preferred duration of antibiotic therapy?
Recommendation: A shorter course of antibiotics (no more than 7 days) is suggested instead of a longer course (more than 7 days) in children with UTI. Selected uncomplicated cystitis cases may require only 3–5 days, while younger or higher-risk children may require longer treatment.
Strength / Quality: Conditional recommendation, Moderate-quality evidence

Q5: What imaging should be performed after first febrile UTI?
Recommendation: Renal and bladder ultrasonography remains recommended after a first febrile UTI to identify structural abnormalities, but routine voiding cystourethrography (VCUG) is no longer advised after a first febrile UTI when ultrasound findings are normal.
Strength / Quality: Strong recommendation (ultrasound); conditional recommendation against routine VCUG

Q6: What is the approach to recurrent UTIs?
Recommendation: If UTIs recur, doctors should consider constipation and bladder-bowel dysfunction as possible contributing factors.
Strength / Quality: Good practice statement

Key change from 2011: Age range expanded from 2 months–2 years to 8 days–5 years; race removed from risk algorithms; culture threshold lowered for catheterized specimens; VCUG no longer routine; shorter antibiotic courses endorsed.

Three accompanying technical reports (open access): https://doi.org/10.1542/peds.2026-078566 | https://doi.org/10.1542/peds.2026-078567 | https://doi.org/10.1542/peds.2026-078568

A Multicenter Evaluation of Gram-Negative Bloodstream Infections in Children: Predictors of Carbapenem Resistance
The Pediatric Infectious Disease Journal, November 2026; 45(11):973–978
https://doi.org/10.1097/INF.0000000000005270
PDF: Paywalled

  • Access: PAYWALLED (checked: Unpaywall/Europe PMC)
  • Design: Multicenter cross-sectional (point prevalence) study; 1-month point prevalence study conducted in November 2023 across 27 tertiary hospitals. Hospitalized patients aged 1 month–18 years with laboratory-confirmed gram-negative bloodstream infections (GNBSIs) were included.
  • Setting: Turkey (27 tertiary hospitals), n=15,249 total patients screened
  • Background: Pediatric carbapenem-resistant gram-negative (CRGN) infections cause high morbidity and mortality. Despite the rising global threat, multicenter data on resistance drivers in children remain limited.
  • Key Findings:
- Among 15,249 patients, 180 GNBSIs were identified (prevalence: 1.19%). K. pneumoniae (28.3%), E. coli (12.7%), and P. aeruginosa (11.5%) were the most frequent pathogens. - The overall carbapenem resistance (CR) rate was 33.3%, peaking at 73.6% in A. baumannii. - Independent risk factors for CRGN BSIs included university hospital setting (aOR 3.05; 95% CI 1.47–6.31), central venous catheterization (aOR 3.23; 95% CI 1.10–9.40), mechanical ventilation (aOR 2.59; 95% CI 1.18–5.63), and steroid use (aOR 2.59; 95% CI 1.10–6.04).
  • Conclusions: This study highlights a high burden of CR in pediatric GNBSIs in Turkey. The identification of modifiable risk factors and the high prevalence of multidrug-resistant isolates emphasize the urgent need for enhanced infection control and robust antimicrobial stewardship programs.
  • Limitations: Single-country (Turkey), high-endemicity setting; 1-month point prevalence (may not capture seasonal or year-to-year variation); no outcome data reported; pediatric-specific antimicrobial susceptibility testing breakpoints not specified. Generalizability to low-endemicity settings (e.g., U.S., Western Europe) is limited.
  • Clinical Implications: CR rates of 33.3% overall and 73.6% for A. baumannii are alarmingly high and reinforce that in high-endemicity settings, empiric coverage for CRGN must be incorporated into initial risk stratification. The four modifiable/identifiable risk factors (CVC, MV, steroids, university hospital) constitute a practical bedside risk score. This extends findings from previous Australian cohort work (reviewed in prior digest) showing that even in low-endemicity settings, empiric activity may not alter outcomes — here, the question of which empiric agent to choose in high-endemicity contexts is critical. Practice-informing for U.S. pediatric ID teams seeing internationally transferred or recently traveled patients.

Procalcitonin- and Presepsin-Guided Antibiotics for Early-Onset Sepsis in Preterm Infants: A Randomized Clinical Trial
JAMA Network Open, November 12, 2026; 9(11):e2643418
https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2026.43418
PDF: https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamanetworkopen/articlepdf/2856575/dani_2026_oi_261167_1793902785.3576.pdf

  • Access: OPEN ACCESS (checked: Unpaywall/Europe PMC)
  • Design: Randomized, open-label, parallel-group, multicenter prospective clinical trial, conducted across 17 neonatal intensive care units in Italy between June 1, 2023, and January 31, 2025. Noninferiority design.
  • Population: The study enrolled and randomized 283 very preterm infants (mean [SD] gestational age, 30.2 [2.2] weeks; 161 girls [57%]); 143 received procalcitonin- and presepsin-guided treatment and 140 received universal antibiotic treatment.
  • Background: Early administration of antibiotics in the absence of proven sepsis, such as for treatment of early-onset sepsis (EOS), has been associated with adverse outcomes in very preterm infants. This trial attempts to address a clinically critical question: can biomarker-guided strategies safely reduce antibiotic exposure in preterm neonates at risk for EOS? The prior NeoPIns RCT demonstrated that PCT-guided duration reduction was safe in term/near-term infants; this trial extends the question to initiation decisions in very preterm neonates.
  • Methods: Intervention: initiation of antibiotics guided by PCT and presepsin (P-SEP) values measured shortly after birth; Comparator: universal antibiotic prophylaxis for all enrolled preterm neonates. Primary endpoint: Survival without major morbidity (composite: death, severe IVH, PVL, BPD, NEC ≥Stage II, ROP ≥Stage III, nosocomial sepsis). Noninferiority margin was prespecified. Open-label design with blinded outcome assessment.
  • Key Findings:
- This randomized clinical trial found that, compared with universal antibiotic treatment, noninferiority of a PCT- and P-SEP–guided strategy for EOS treatment with respect to survival without major morbidity in very preterm infants was not demonstrated. - In plain terms: the biomarker-guided group did not meet the prespecified noninferiority margin for the primary composite outcome. The trial therefore does not support biomarker-guided antibiotic initiation decision-making for EOS in very preterm neonates as a replacement for universal treatment.
  • Discussion: This is a critical negative trial with major clinical implications. While the concept of biomarker-guided stewardship is appealing — and prior work supports PCT-guided duration reduction in term infants — this trial demonstrates that withholding or delaying antibiotics based on PCT/P-SEP levels in very preterm neonates at risk for EOS is not yet validated. The unique vulnerability of this population (immature immune response, rapid deterioration with bacterial sepsis, high baseline mortality risk from co-morbidities) may explain why adult/term-infant biomarker approaches do not translate. This should be compared with the Netherlands PRESAFE trial design (ongoing), which uses a different presepsin cut-off and population.
  • Limitations:
1. Open-label design — most threatening limitation: unblinded clinicians may have differed in antibiotic decisions beyond the protocol, potentially disadvantaging the intervention arm (cite: The open-label design may have influenced clinical management, including decisions regarding antibiotic initiation and discontinuation, despite blinded assessment of major outcomes.) 2. Single-country (Italy): NICU practices, baseline pathogen epidemiology, and culture rates differ meaningfully from U.S. and other settings 3. EOS incidence was 0 in both arms — suggesting a potentially low-risk or low-incidence population, which raises the question of whether the trial was adequately powered to detect safety signals specifically related to missed EOS cases, vs. other components of the composite morbidity endpoint 4. Very preterm infants (mean GA 30.2 weeks) represent a heterogeneous group; results may not apply to extremely preterm (<28 weeks) neonates
  • Clinical Implications: Practice-confirming of current standard: universal empiric antibiotic coverage for high-risk preterm neonates at risk for EOS remains appropriate. Biomarker-guided cessation (once cultures confirmed negative after 36–48 hours) remains supported by other evidence. This trial does not support biomarker-guided initiation decisions in very preterm EOS risk. Any institution contemplating pilot programs using PCT/P-SEP to withhold empiric antibiotics in preterm EOS-risk neonates should not proceed based on this evidence.

Complications in Pediatric Cancer Patients With Staphylococcus aureus Bloodstream Infection
Pediatrics, November 4, 2026; e2025075263
https://doi.org/10.1542/peds.2025-075263
PDF: Paywalled

  • Access: PAYWALLED
  • Design: Multicenter retrospective cohort, n not specified in abstract; multicenter U.S. (13 centers, including Vanderbilt-Ingram Cancer Center)
  • Background: Staphylococcus aureus bloodstream infections (BSIs) can cause significant morbidity in children with cancer. Central venous catheters are ubiquitous in pediatric oncology patients, and SAB in this population carries substantial risk of metastatic complications, prolonged bacteremia, and mortality. Optimal management — particularly catheter removal — remains controversial given the clinical consequences of CVC removal in this population.

Practice-informing.

Use of Integrase Strand Transfer Inhibitors Among Children Living With HIV in Latin America and the Caribbean
The Pediatric Infectious Disease Journal, November 2026; 45(11):979–985
https://doi.org/10.1097/INF.0000000000005281
PDF: https://doi.org/10.1097/inf.0000000000005281

  • Access: OPEN ACCESS (checked: Unpaywall/Europe PMC)
  • Design: Retrospective multicenter cohort using Caribbean, Central and South America Network for HIV Epidemiology (CCASAnet) data; approximately 2.5 million people are living with HIV in Latin America, including 31,000 children under the age of 15 years, with around 3800 new infections occurring annually in this age group. Three-country analysis (specific countries not disclosed in abstract).
  • Background: Limited data exist on integrase strand transfer inhibitor (INSTI) use among children. Authors assessed dolutegravir (DTG) and raltegravir (RAL) use and outcomes among Latin American and Caribbean children with HIV.
  • Methods: Descriptive analysis of ART-naïve children initiating therapy, and time-to-event analyses for INSTI initiation among ART-experienced children. Multivariable logistic and Cox regression models examined trends and factors associated with DTG/RAL initiation. Comparisons of virologic suppression rates between INSTI and non-INSTI regimens.
  • Key Findings:
- Among ART-naïve children, 77/110 started DTG, 5/109 started RAL, and 91 started other ART. The proportion of ART-naïve children initiating DTG increased from 0% in 2016 to 93% (13/14) in 2022. - Among ART-experienced children, 122/273 switched to DTG, and 25/288 switched to RAL. - There was no statistical difference in virologic suppression among ART-naïve children by use or non-use of DTG; however, children started on DTG had a longer duration of the first regimen than those started on other ART (P < 0.01). - The study highlights the successful integration and increasing use of DTG and rare use of RAL in pediatric HIV treatment in 3 Latin American and Caribbean countries.
  • Discussion: The rapid uptake of DTG (0% → 93% in naïve children over 6 years) reflects global WHO guidance favoring DTG as first-line across age groups. The finding of longer first-regimen durability with DTG aligns with adult data and is consistent with DTG's higher barrier to resistance. No statistical difference in virologic suppression may reflect insufficient power (n=110 ART-naïve), heterogeneity in baseline viral loads, or limited follow-up. The low RAL use is notable given WHO recommendations to transition away from RAL-based regimens in children when DTG is available.
  • Limitations:
1. Three-country regional analysis — not generalizable globally or even across all of Latin America 2. Relatively small numbers, particularly in ART-naïve DTG group (n=77), limiting power for virologic outcome comparisons 3. Missing data: CD4% missing in 83 children in some analyses; HIV RNA missing in 81 children; among ART-experienced children, CD4% missing in 177. Substantial missingness threatens the virologic outcome conclusions 4. Retrospective design with heterogeneous follow-up duration across sites 5. Future multisite studies focused on young children with HIV and receiving DTG are urgently needed — the authors acknowledge the evidence base remains thin
  • Clinical Implications: Confirms real-world DTG uptake trajectory in Latin America and Caribbean pediatric HIV care. The longer regimen durability signal is reassuring and supports continued DTG-first strategies. Clinicians caring for children with perinatally acquired HIV from these regions should be aware of ongoing INSTI-based regimen transitions and monitor for emerging resistance mutations (see prior digest review of INSTI resistance in Northeast Brazil). Practice-informing.

Current Prognostic Models Fail to Reliably Distinguish Kawasaki Disease From Pediatric Multisystem Inflammatory Syndrome Temporally Associated With COVID-19
The Pediatric Infectious Disease Journal, November 2026; 45(11):942–946
https://doi.org/10.1097/INF.0000000000005277
PDF: https://doi.org/10.1097/inf.0000000000005277

  • Access: OPEN ACCESS (checked: Unpaywall/Europe PMC)
  • Design: External validation study of two published prognostic scoring models; two externally developed prognostic models were applied to 2 distinct datasets: one consisting solely of 26 KD and PIMS cases from Bayreuth, the other comprising 632 cases of both diagnoses reported by pediatricians from all over Germany and Innsbruck, Austria.
  • Setting: Germany and Austria, multicenter
  • Background: Differentiating Kawasaki Disease (KD) from MIS-C (PIMS-TS) remains clinically challenging, particularly as overlap in inflammatory features is substantial. Several scoring models have been proposed using routine clinical and laboratory parameters. The critical question for clinicians: can any existing model reliably replace clinical gestalt in guiding treatment (IVIG vs. steroids vs. biologics)?
  • Methods: The selection of data was determined by the parameters utilized in the models: parameters considered by Starnes et al. included age, serum sodium, C-reactive protein (CRP), alanine-aminotransferase (ALT), blood platelets and LVEF. Sensitivity and specificity of each model calculated; κ-coefficients by Cohen and correlation analysis performed. Dataset 1 (n=26): single-center local cohort. Dataset 2 (n=632): nationwide pediatrician-reported cases.
  • Key Findings:
- The sensitivity and specificity of both prognostic models did not display a reliable distinguishability between KD and PIMS. Moreover, correlation analysis cannot confirm the correlation of most parameters utilized in the models with the PIMS diagnosis. The only parameter showing a positive correlation in both datasets was the patients' older age. - In plain terms: neither model validated well enough to be clinically actionable. Older age remains the most consistent distinguishing feature (MIS-C tends to present in older children; KD peaks in toddlers).
  • Discussion: This study provides important — if humbling — validation data showing that existing KD/MIS-C discriminating models do not perform well externally. The finding is consistent with the clinical reality that KD and MIS-C share substantial pathophysiologic overlap, particularly in the "atypical KD" phenotype and in MIS-C with coronary involvement. The authors emphasize that the current clinical syndromic definitions drive treatment decisions better than any available scoring model. This contrasts with a companion paper in PIDJ (Ljubas et al., 2026, DOI 10.1097/INF.0000000000005208) that identified mitral regurgitation as a potential differentiating feature for MIS-C.
  • Limitations:
1. Dataset 1 (n=26) is severely underpowered and limits any generalization from the single-center arm 2. The larger dataset (n=632) relied on pediatrician-reported diagnoses without centralized confirmation — diagnostic misclassification bias is a real concern and may artificially inflate model failure 3. Models were developed in specific populations and may require recalibration for different epidemiologic contexts 4. Retrospective design; no prospective clinical utility assessment
  • Clinical Implications: Do not rely on existing KD/MIS-C discriminating scores alone to guide management decisions. Clinical synthesis — including age, timing (days after COVID illness), echocardiographic findings, and inflammatory marker pattern — remains the standard. Mitral regurgitation on echo may be an emerging differentiator worth prospective study. Practice-confirming of current approach (multidisciplinary clinical assessment over algorithmic scoring).

Beyond Croup: A Phenotype-Based Perspective on Parainfluenza Virus Types in Children
Pediatrics, State-of-the-Art Review | November 13, 2026; e2026078012
https://doi.org/10.1542/peds.2026-078012
PDF: Paywalled

  • Access: PAYWALLED (checked: Unpaywall/Europe PMC)
  • Design: State-of-the-Art Review (not a primary study)
  • Background: Human parainfluenza viruses (PIVs) caused an estimated 18.8 million acute lower respiratory infection episodes, 725,000 hospitalizations, and 34,400 deaths among children under 5 in 2018, ranking second only to RSV. Despite this burden, clinical attention has historically focused on croup, leading to the common perception that parainfluenza is a single disease entity.
  • Scope: PIV-1, the most common cause of croup, drives biennial epidemics in toddlers, whereas PIV-2 is less frequent but can cause severe disease, particularly in children with underlying conditions. PIV infections are most severe in infants, immunocompromised children, and those in low-resource settings. This review reframes parainfluenza as a phenotypically diverse group of viruses with distinct epidemiology, severity patterns, and host risk factor profiles beyond the "croup" label.
  • Clinical Implications: As multiplex respiratory panels become standard in clinical diagnosis, parainfluenza type-level differentiation is now achievable. This review helps clinicians understand what a positive PIV-3 means (bronchiolitis/pneumonia in infants) vs. PIV-1/2 (laryngotracheobronchitis in toddlers) vs. PIV-4 (rarely severe in immunocompetent hosts). Practice-informing for clinical risk stratification and informing parent counseling. Relevant to respiratory season now underway.

Postnatal Care–Associated Echovirus 11 Infection in Malaysia

https://journals.lww.com/pidj/pages/default.aspx
PDF: Paywalled

  • Access: PAYWALLED (checked: Unpaywall/Europe PMC)
  • Design: Case series/outbreak report; Malaysia, single- or multi-center postnatal care setting
  • Background: Echovirus 11 (E11) is a common circulating enterovirus globally and in Malaysia. A Case Series of Vaccine-Strain Reactivation Varicella Zoster in Two Pediatric Patients Presenting With Headache and Rash
The Pediatric Infectious Disease Journal, November 2026; 45(11):e449–e451
https://journals.lww.com/pidj/pages/default.aspx
PDF: Paywalled

  • Access: PAYWALLED (checked: Unpaywall/Europe PMC)
  • Design: Case series, n=2; U.S. (Eberly, Manaloor — likely Indiana University/Riley Hospital based on authorship)
  • Key Finding: Two cases of vaccine-strain reactivation varicella zoster virus meningitis occurred in immunocompetent pediatric patients who received routine vaccination series and presented with headache and rash.
  • Background: Vaccine-strain VZV (Oka strain) reactivation causing herpes zoster and rarely zoster meningitis in immunocompetent children is a known but rare phenomenon estimated at approximately 1–2/100,000 vaccine recipients. It is distinguishable from wild-type VZV reactivation only by genetic analysis (Oka-strain genotyping by PCR). Most clinicians may not consider this diagnosis in a vaccinated child with aseptic meningitis.
  • Clinical Implications: In vaccinated children presenting with headache, rash (dermatomal or maculopapular), and CSF pleocytosis, vaccine-strain VZV reactivation should be on the differential. CSF VZV PCR should be sent; if positive, Oka-strain genotyping (available at CDC and some reference labs) confirms vaccine vs. wild-type origin. Treatment with acyclovir is appropriate; corticosteroids remain controversial. Hypothesis-generating for the magnitude of this phenomenon; reinforces value of VZV CSF testing in pediatric aseptic meningitis.

Hantavirus Outbreak Caused by Andes Virus Linked to International Cruise Ship Travel — United States, 2026
MMWR Morbidity and Mortality Weekly Report, November 5, 2026; 75(43):632–639
https://doi.org/10.15585/mmwr.mm7543a1
PDF: https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/75/wr/pdfs/mm7543a1-H.pdf

  • Access: OPEN ACCESS (checked: Unpaywall/Europe PMC)
  • Design: Outbreak investigation report; in April 2026, an outbreak of hantavirus infection caused by the Andes virus was identified on the Dutch cruise ship MV Hondius.
  • Background: Andes virus (ANDV) is endemic to South America (Argentina, Chile) and is unique among hantaviruses as the only species with documented person-to-person transmission.   • Key Findings:
- As of May 15, 2026, former cruise ship passengers were hospitalized or quarantined in Australia, Canada, France, Germany, the Netherlands, Saint Helena, Singapore, South Africa, Spain, Switzerland, Turkey, and the United States. - The 2026 MV Hondius cruise resulted in 11 confirmed hantavirus cases and 3 deaths. - Serologic testing was completed for 27 contacts, including 18 cruise passengers, and nine contacts who developed symptoms during monitoring.   • Pediatric Relevance: While this outbreak primarily affected adults, the differential diagnosis of ANDV HPS must be considered in any pediatric patient who traveled to Patagonia in the past 1–8 weeks presenting with prodromal febrile illness, bilateral pulmonary infiltrates, thrombocytopenia, and elevated hematocrit. ANDV HPS can present in adolescents engaged in ecotourism/hiking. No pediatric cases were confirmed in this outbreak.
  • Clinical Implications: Any febrile illness with respiratory deterioration and thrombocytopenia in a returning Patagonia traveler requires urgent hantavirus testing. Testing is available through state health departments and CDC (RT-PCR, serology). No antiviral therapy (ribavirin) has proven efficacy for ANDV HPS; supportive care in ICU is the mainstay. Alert your travel medicine and emergency teams. Practice-informing for travel medicine and ID consultation.

Emerging Infectious Diseases, November 2026; 32(11)
https://wwwnc.cdc.gov/eid/article/32/11/26-1019_article
PDF: https://wwwnc.cdc.gov/eid/article/32/11/pdfs/26-1019.pdf (Open Access)

  • Access: OPEN ACCESS
  • Design: Perspective/outbreak analysis; Bhuiya S et al., Emerging Infectious Diseases
  • Background: During March 15–September 13, 2026, Bangladesh reported 170,638 suspected and 20,006 confirmed measles cases, including 919 deaths among suspected and 100 deaths among laboratory-confirmed cases.
  • Key Findings:
- The outbreak was overwhelmingly pediatric. 81% of cases were in children under five, including 34% in infants under nine months — a group not yet eligible for routine measles vaccination. - As of November 15, 2026, 1,414 new suspected measles cases were reported in a single 24-hour period in Bangladesh, with cumulative suspected cases reaching 248,060 and confirmed cases reaching 25,269. - The 2026 measles outbreak was not merely a biological phenomenon driven by a highly contagious virus; it was a systems-sensitive emergency that exposed the intersection of vaccination disruption resulting from political upheaval, nutritional neglect, disruption of regular vaccine procurement, perceived adverse effects of COVID-19 vaccination, widespread misconceptions, and the long-term disruption of routine immunization services. - Bangladesh's measles-rubella vaccine stocks were exhausted in March 2026, followed by a sharp decline in vaccination coverage.
  • Discussion: The Bangladesh outbreak is the starkest contemporary illustration of how rapidly measles herd immunity collapses when routine immunization infrastructure is disrupted — a lesson directly applicable to the ongoing U.S. outbreak. The Bangladesh case also demonstrates how nosocomial amplification becomes a force multiplier in resource-constrained settings. Connecting this to the U.S. context: once elimination status is lost, the pathway back requires >12 months of interrupted transmission.
  • Clinical Implications: This perspective reinforces the urgency of immunization advocacy. For clinicians caring for refugees or immigrants from Bangladesh (or neighboring countries), measles screening and catch-up vaccination should be prioritized on first contact. Practice-informing and contextually essential for understanding the global measles threat.

Cutaneous Aspergillosis in Extremely Preterm Neonates — Michigan and New York, 2023–2024

MMWR Morbidity and Mortality Weekly Report, November 5, 2026; 75(43) (Full MMWR text — full DOI pending; listed in MMWR No. 43 current issue) Link: https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/index2026.html

Access: OPEN ACCESS

This MMWR report describes two clusters of cutaneous aspergillosis in neonatal intensive care units (NICUs) — one in Michigan (October–November 2023) and one in New York (July–September 2024) — investigated by their respective state health departments with CDC involvement.

Michigan cluster (n = 2): Both patients were extremely preterm (gestational age [GA] <24 weeks) and developed skin lesions during the first week of life. Cultures and PCR/ITS sequencing confirmed Aspergillus fumigatus in both cases. Both received amphotericin B. The first infant died during the second week of life from septic shock attributed to aspergillosis, Pseudomonas species co-infection, and extreme prematurity; the second infant survived (case fatality: 1/2, 50%). Environmental sampling identified A. fumigatus in one air sample collected near a corridor used for linen transport — the only environmental positive, and no definitive source was established. Mitigation measures included converting negative-pressure areas in the shared nursery to positive pressure, repairing plumbing leaks and flooring, and designating a separate loading dock for clean linens.

New York cluster (n = 3): Three neonates were affected — two at GA 24–25 weeks and one at GA <24 weeks — all presenting with skin lesions during the first or second week of life. State public health laboratory ITS sequencing confirmed A. fumigatus in the first isolate; the second and third isolates were reclassified as Aspergillus quadrilineatus. Whole-genome sequencing indicated the two A. quadrilineatus isolates were closely related, suggesting a potential common source. Treatment varied by patient: micafungin (patient 1), voriconazole plus amphotericin B (patient 2), and amphotericin B alone (patient 3). All three survived (case fatality: 0/3, 0%). No environmental sampling was performed; no construction or specific environmental concern was identified on facility assessment. Mitigation included additional linen-dryness checks, replacement of some laundered linens with sterile towels, disposable positioning devices for extremely preterm neonates, sterile gloves for care during the first 4 days of life, chlorhexidine-gluconate–impregnated surgical scrubs for staff and visitors, review of disinfectant products, air-handling system inspection, and proactive replacement of HEPA filters.

As of September 1, 2026, no additional cases had been identified in either NICU.

Limitations acknowledged by the authors: No definitive environmental source was identified in either cluster, consistent with many healthcare-associated mold outbreak investigations. Culture-based case definition may have led to undercounting given variability in culture timing and yield. Because multiple mitigation measures were implemented simultaneously, the effectiveness of any individual intervention cannot be assessed.

Clinical/IPC Action: Clinicians should consider cutaneous aspergillosis when evaluating erythematous, ulcerative, necrotic, or otherwise suspicious skin lesions in extremely preterm neonates (<28 weeks GA). Prompt culture or biopsy of suspicious lesions, species-level identification (with relatedness assessment via whole-genome sequencing when available), and multidisciplinary coordination among clinicians, infection preventionists, facility engineers, environmental services, and public health agencies are essential. The authors emphasize that mitigation should not be delayed while awaiting identification of a definitive environmental source. Antifungal choices in this series included amphotericin B (all Michigan patients; NY patients 2 and 3), micafungin (NY patient 1), and combination voriconazole plus amphotericin B (NY patient 2), reflecting center-level variability in approach. Linen handling, airflow optimization, and enhanced skin-assessment protocols are actionable environmental controls even in the absence of a confirmed source.

Ongoing Bundibugyo Ebola Virus Outbreak — DRC: Updated Status

Federal Register / Public Health Order, November 13, 2026

The incubation period for Ebola disease caused by the Bundibugyo virus is typically between 2 and 21 days, with most people developing symptoms within 4 to 10 days after exposure. The risk of Bundibugyo virus disease introduction into the United States is heightened by the virus's incubation period, which can extend up to 21 days, allowing infected persons to travel internationally while asymptomatic and therefore unlikely to be detected through routine symptom-based screening measures.

A federal public health order continuing suspension of entry for covered persons from affected countries was issued November 13, 2026 (Federal Register 2026-23114). Clinicians should maintain awareness that the DRC outbreak remains ongoing and uncontrolled, with no approved BDBV-specific vaccine or antiviral.

Reminder for pediatric teams: Any child presenting with fever and hemorrhagic features with DRC travel history within 21 days requires immediate airborne + contact + droplet isolation and public health notification before diagnostic workup.
